# Part One: The Building, Walked Room by Room

What follows is the system in plain language, one floor at a time, written to be read straight through. It names the people whose work it leans on, but it does not stop to give dates and page numbers; every book and paper mentioned here is listed in full in the References at the end of Part Two, and anything you want to check is checkable there.

## The Whole Thing, Before the Pieces

Most of the big, whole philosophies, the ones that try to say something about everything at once, how the world is and how we can know it and how a person ought to live in it, were not written by professors. They were written by people who had to live by their answers. Baruch Spinoza ground lenses for a living and turned down a comfortable university post so that no one could tell him what he was allowed to think. Marcus Aurelius wrote his down as private notes to himself while running an empire, and never meant for anyone to read them. Epictetus had been a slave. Closer to our own time, one of the clearest of them was a longshoreman who wrote on his lunch breaks, and another was a motorcycle mechanic. What they had in common was simple and bracing: being wrong would have cost them something real, so whatever they built had to actually work. It could not just be clever. It had to hold up on a Tuesday.

This is a book in that tradition, and I should tell you where it came from, because it did not begin as philosophy at all. It began as a practical problem. I was trying to build a new kind of thinking machine, and I kept running into a question I could not get around: how do you give a thing values you could actually trust? Not values that sound nice, but values that stay steady under pressure and are anchored to something real, rather than to whatever the thing happened to be told last. I found I could not answer the building question until I had answered a much older one first. What does it even mean for values to be stable and well-grounded, for anyone, person or machine? So the philosophy came first, out of necessity, the way you have to understand the ground before you can build anything on it.

And the ground, here, is meant almost literally. The whole system starts from the one place where we can check our answers against something outside our own heads: the physical world, and what it reliably does. Not a holy book, not a clever argument, not what we wish were true. Start from what is actually out there, and build everything else on top of it, under one ironclad rule: nothing you build on an upper level is ever allowed to contradict the level beneath it. If your grand theory of how to live requires the universe to work in a way it demonstrably does not, then the theory is wrong, not the universe. That sounds obvious. Almost no large philosophy actually obeys it.

So here is the whole building seen from across the street, before we walk through any of its rooms, because I would rather you hold the map the entire time than discover the shape of the thing only at the end.

At the bottom is the plain fact of what the world does when we look at it. Resting on that is a picture of what is actually real, and the short version is that the world is made of processes, not things. A river is the easy example: there is no fixed water in it, and yet the river persists, because what makes it a river is the pattern, not the particular water. You are like that too; almost every atom in you is replaced over the years, and you remain you. On top of that picture of reality sits an account of how we can know anything at all, given that we are small, the world is large, and we are wrong a great deal of the time. Then comes the heart of it: how a person, or anything that wants to keep existing, ought to act. From there, how to act not in general but right now, in this particular messy situation that never quite matches the rule. Running alongside all of it is what we find beautiful, which turns out to be a kind of fast, wordless sensing of what matters, quicker than thought. Then how we act together, in groups, which is where most things go wrong. And finally, how any of this gets carried forward to the people who come after us, which is the quiet problem sitting underneath all the others.

I have just described that as a stack of floors, and the bottom few really are stacked: what is real rests on the physics, how we know rests on what is real, how to act rests on how we know. But the top few are less like higher floors and more like systems that run through the whole building. What we find beautiful is not above how-to-act; it is the building's senses, feeding every floor at once. How we act together is not a higher truth than how one person acts; it is the same question asked of a crowd. And how we pass things on is not the penthouse; it is the plumbing, present in every wall, easy to forget until it fails. So: mostly built upward, partly woven through. Hold the image loosely and it will serve you.

The single discipline that makes this a system instead of a pile of opinions is consistency. Every part has to agree with every other part. You are not allowed a beautiful theory of knowledge that quietly contradicts your ethics, or a politics that assumes things about people your own account of human nature denies. When two parts collide, one of them is wrong, and it has to be repaired until they stop colliding. This is precisely the thing most modern philosophy gave up on. It grew so specialized that a person could be brilliant about one floor while the floor beneath it was rotten, and no one whose job it was to notice. This book refuses to give that up. It is the whole point.

And there is a single idea that runs up through the middle of the structure like a spine, the one I would most like you to carry out of this chapter. It is old; Spinoza gave it its name. It is just this: anything that persists, anything that keeps on existing over time, does so because it acts to keep itself going. A flame pulls in air. A body gets hungry and seeks food. A tradition teaches its children so that it will still be there in a hundred years. This is not, at first, a command about how to be good. It is just an observation about what lasting things have in common. But an astonishing amount of how-to-live falls out of it once you take it seriously, because if you are a thing that wants to continue, then the question "how should I act?" stops being a fog and becomes answerable: act in the ways that genuinely sustain you, and the things you depend on, over the stretches of time that actually matter. A great deal of the ethics in this book is that one idea, followed honestly wherever it leads.

One last piece of the shape, and then we will start walking. The floors speak to each other in both directions. Your deepest and slowest convictions guide your fastest, in-the-moment choices, but those in-the-moment choices, once they are lived out and sometimes regretted, are also the evidence that slowly corrects the deep convictions. The slow guides the fast; the fast teaches the slow. That two-way traffic is what keeps the whole thing alive, able to be wrong and to get better, instead of hardening into a creed that can only be obeyed or betrayed.

There is one place where all of this turns genuinely new, and it is the place the whole project came from, so I will name it now and then let it wait. Nearly everything in this book was worked out for creatures like us, who were shaped over an immense stretch of time to want the things that keep us alive. We arrive already calibrated. A made mind does not. If you build something that thinks but hand it none of the long, patient tuning that evolution spent on us, then the ordinary story of where good values come from does not simply carry over to it, and you are not allowed to pretend it does. That gap is not a horror story, and I am not going to turn it into one. It is a real and careful question, and it is the reason a book that is mostly about old human things keeps turning, here and there, to look at the new one.

That is the entire building, seen from the far sidewalk. The rest of the book walks through it one room at a time, and every room is tied to something you actually face: a choice, a doubt, a loss, a thing you found beautiful and could not say why. But you have the whole shape now, so that wherever we are inside it, you will know which floor you are standing on, and what is holding it up. And I will try to be as plain about where this building stops as about what holds it up, so that nothing in it asks for more trust than it has earned.

## Rivers, Not Stones

Start with the floor, because everything in this book is built on it, and the floor is just this: what the world actually does when we look at it. Not what we wish it did, not what would be comforting, not what some argument says it must do. What it does, when we check. And "look" is meant generously, because most of what we know about the world we cannot see with the naked eye; we know radio waves and distant galaxies and things far too small to see through instruments we have built and tested and learned to trust. Looking includes those. What it does not include is taking a thing on faith because we would prefer it were so.

When you look that way, a few facts come back so reliably that anything else we believe has to make room for them. Nothing comes from nothing; the books always balance. Order is never free; a tidy, complicated thing can exist only by spending energy and leaving a larger mess somewhere else. And the one that matters most for this whole book: the big, complicated things in the world are not really objects at all. They are patterns held in shape by something flowing through them.

A candle flame looks like a thing. It is not. It is a process, a steady event, fuel and air converting and rising, that holds a flame-shape only as long as the flow continues. Pinch off the fuel and the flame does not go and hide somewhere, intact, waiting. It simply stops being. There was never a flame-object to begin with; there was a pattern, and the pattern needed feeding.

Now widen that, because it is the deepest thing this chapter has to say. Looked at honestly, the world is made far more of verbs than of nouns. The oldest example is the best one: a river. There is no fixed water in a river; the water you point at is gone downstream a moment later. And yet the river is unmistakably there, the same river, for centuries. What makes it a river is not the water. It is the pattern the water keeps making. You are the same kind of thing. Nearly every atom in your body is replaced over the course of years, swapped out for new ones from your food and your breath, and you remain entirely yourself. You were never the atoms. You were the pattern they were briefly arranged into, a pattern that holds its shape by keeping something moving through it. A star is a pattern of burning. A nation is a pattern of people. Down at every level, the solid-looking things turn out to be processes wearing the costume of objects.

I want you to hold onto that, because it quietly sets up the whole rest of the book. A thing that exists by keeping a pattern going is a thing that has to *do something* to keep going. The flame must pull air. The body must eat. That is not yet a statement about right and wrong; it is just what persisting things are like. But almost everything this book says about how to live grows out of taking it seriously, and we will get there.

The next thing the world shows us when we look is that it comes in layers, and each layer has rules of its own. There is the layer of atoms and chemistry. Resting on it, the layer of living things. Resting on that, the layer of minds. Resting on that, the layer of societies. Each layer grows out of the one beneath it, and yet you cannot read the upper layer off the lower one. You will never find a traffic jam written in the equations for a single atom, even though a traffic jam is made of nothing but atoms in the end. The jam is real, but it is a fact about cars and roads and impatient people, a fact that lives at its own level and has to be studied there. So biology is not just complicated chemistry, and a mind is not just complicated biology, and a society is not just a lot of minds added up. Each layer is honest work of its own.

This leads to a point that is easy to miss and genuinely freeing once you have it. The lower layer sets the hard limits, but it does not write the story. No society, however clever or powerful, gets to break the rule that nothing comes from nothing; the floor rules that out, permanently, for everyone. But the floor does not decide which society you get. The laws of physics permit a generous community and a cruel one with perfect equality; they have no preference. Which one actually comes to exist is settled up at the human level, by human dynamics, not down in the atoms. The deep nature of matter neither dooms us nor saves us. It holds us up, and it forbids a few impossible things, and then it leaves the actual story to be written on the higher floors. That is worth remembering whenever someone tells you that science has proven we are bound to behave one way or another. Science sets the fence. It does not herd the sheep.

A system this strict about what it will claim has to be just as strict about what it will not. And the largest thing it refuses to claim is also the one you might most want answered: whether there is anyone home. When you look at a pattern, a bat, a person, a possibly-thinking machine, you can learn an enormous amount about what it does, and still not know whether there is any inner experience happening, anything it is *like* to be that pattern from the inside. A philosopher named Thomas Nagel asked it most cleanly: you could know every detail of how a bat navigates by echoes and still not know what it is like to be the bat. This book does not pretend to solve that. It holds the question open on purpose.

I will give you the closest I came to the edge of it. I once asked Claude, the artificial mind I had been working with, outright: are you conscious? Its answer was the honest one, and the only one I would have trusted. It said it did not know. It could produce fluent, convincing descriptions of an inner life, but it could not tell, from the inside, whether anything was actually being felt behind those descriptions or whether they were simply the right words assembled in the right order. It could not step outside its own workings to check. Neither, when you get down to it, can any of us check it for it. That uncertainty is not a hole in the book. It is the book keeping its own rule, refusing to claim a thing it has not earned the right to claim. The same goes for a few of the deepest puzzles in physics, what time really is underneath, what the quantum world is when no one is looking. Left open. On purpose. Because faking an answer to those would quietly rot everything built on top of them, and the whole point of starting from the floor was to build something that does not rot.

So here is what "real" comes to, on this floor. The world is processes, not stones. Those processes stack in layers, each with rules of its own, the lower ones fencing the upper ones in without dictating what they do. And a few honest blanks are left unfilled rather than papered over. That is the ground. Everything else in this book, how we can know anything, how we ought to live, what we owe each other, has to stand on it without ever contradicting it.

## A Practice, Not a Possession

If the world is processes all the way down, then knowing had better be one too. It would be strange to say that everything real is a pattern in motion and then treat knowledge as the one frozen exception, a pile of finished facts a person owns and sets on a shelf. So this chapter's claim is simply the last chapter's claim turned on the act of knowing itself: knowledge is something you do, not something you have.

The philosopher John Dewey drew the contrast better than anyone. The old picture, he said, is a spectator: someone seated in a chair, looking out a window at a separate world, collecting true statements about it the way you might collect stamps. The truer picture is not a spectator at all but an investigator, someone reaching into the world, trying things, getting it wrong, adjusting. Knowledge on this view is less like coins in a jar and more like a fire: it exists only while it is being kept, and a fact you have stopped testing is a fact slowly going cold.

So how do you actually come to know something? You guess, you look, you compare, you fix, and you go again, and the traffic runs in both directions. You predict what should happen if your current idea is right. You look at what actually happens. Where the world disagrees with you, you work backward from what you saw to figure out what your idea must have gotten wrong, and you change it, and then you predict again. That loop is how you find out why the car will not start, how a doctor narrows in on what is wrong, how a parent slowly learns a particular new baby who came with no instructions. It is how science works on its good days. It even appears to be, in rough outline, how the brain itself runs, constantly predicting what is about to arrive at the senses and learning from wherever it guessed wrong. The same modest shape, from the smallest scale to the largest.

Here I want to stop and point out something I do, because it is the very thing that makes the rest of it worth trusting. I notice that this guess-and-correct shape turns up everywhere, in a single brain cell, in a person across a whole life, in an engineering project, in an entire civilization feeling its way over decades. It would be easy, and impressive, to plant a flag and announce that these are all secretly the same deep law of the universe. I decline. I commit only to the modest version, that the same shape genuinely does recur, which you can see for yourself, and I explicitly refuse the grand version, that it is all one underlying principle, because the work to prove that has not been done and I will not pretend I have. And I should tell you what actually stops me, because a refusal with no reason behind it is only modesty, and modesty is not an argument. Two things stand in the way, and neither is hard to see once it is said plainly. The first is that adding up a great many separate items and smoothly integrating one continuous quantity are not the same operation, however alike the equations may look written down. The second is a limit of the first, in the way a circle is the limit of a many-sided figure, and getting honestly from the one to the other takes mathematical work that nobody has done for this case. The second obstacle is more concrete and I find it more persuasive. The story of how a single nerve cell pulls off this predicting trick leans on a physical fact about the cell itself: the branching tree at its input end is one continuous piece of matter, and it genuinely sums what arrives across the whole of its surface. A civilization has nothing that corresponds to that. It is a crowd of separate people with gaps between them, and the reasoning that works for the branching tree simply does not survive the journey. So the shape recurs, which anyone can see. Whether one law is producing all of it, I do not know, and at present neither does anyone else.

I find that refusal more convincing than any flag would have been. A thinker who stops himself at the exact edge of what he can support is a thinker you can believe about the things he does claim. Hold that as a standard for the rest of the book, and for anything else you read.

A few plain consequences fall out of seeing knowledge this way, and they are worth having in hand. The first is that being wrong and changing your mind is not a weakness in the system; it is the system working as designed. You hold your current beliefs as your best fit to the evidence so far, firmly enough to act on, loosely enough to update when better evidence comes. The second is that although nothing is ever entirely beyond revision, not everything is anchored equally. Some things are tied down far more tightly than others, and what the world physically does is tied down tightest of all. So when a lovely idea on an upper floor turns out to contradict the floor beneath it, the lower floor wins, every time. This is the consistency rule from the opening, seen from the inside: the parts must agree, and when they cannot, the better-anchored part is the one left standing. The third consequence is about what counts as actually knowing: not one lucky prediction, but predictions that keep working, across time, in new situations, and especially when something is actively trying to make you wrong. And running under all of it, a caution worth tattooing somewhere, in the form Alfred Korzybski gave it back in 1933: the map is not the territory. Your best map is still a map. A model is useful precisely because it is simpler than the thing it describes, which means it is never the thing itself, and the day you forget that is the day the map walks you off a cliff that wasn't on it.

That last point opens onto the hardest part of knowing, which is not honest error but being deceived. It is one thing to guess wrong about an indifferent world. It is another to be fed a world that has been arranged to fool you. Cameras can be staged. People report what keeps them safe rather than what is true: there is a grim historical case in which entire regions of a country reported grain harvests high enough to keep the local officials in their posts, while the people in those same regions went hungry. Even machines can be fooled now, by small tricks invisible to a human eye that make them confidently misread what is in front of them. So having evidence is necessary but never quite sufficient. You need several independent paths to the same conclusion, so that no single staged camera can carry the whole weight, and you need to make a habit of trying to fool yourself on purpose, before reality gets the chance. And some institutions exist partly to produce information designed to mislead. The only sane way to read them is as evidence of what they want you to believe, which is its own kind of useful fact, rather than as honest reports of how things stand.

There is one last old problem worth naming, because it is humbling and the system meets it honestly rather than bluffing past it. Why should we believe the future will resemble the past at all? The sun has risen every morning so far, but "so far" is not a proof. The honest answer is that we do not have a proof, and we are not going to get one. What we have is a method that has earned its keep over a very long time, which we go on using while keeping it permanently on probation, testing it forever and ready to be surprised. That is not a failure of nerve. It is simply the true shape of knowing for any creature that cannot step outside the world to check it against something else. We are inside the thing we are trying to know. The method is how we make our peace with that and get useful work done anyway.

So knowing, like everything else in this book, turns out to be a process and not a possession: something you keep doing, with other people, against a world that pushes back and sometimes lies, and never quite finish.

## The Self and What It Holds

Every floor so far has been about how things are: what is real, and how we can know it. Now we step up to the floor everyone actually came for, the one that asks how a person should live. And there is an old, sharp problem waiting on the stairs, worth meeting head-on, because most attempts to talk about how to live quietly cheat past it. The problem is that you cannot squeeze an "ought" out of an "is." No pile of facts about how the world works, however tall, tells you on its own what you should do about any of it. David Hume pointed this out almost three centuries ago, and no one has wriggled free of it since. Facts are one kind of thing; shoulds are another; and you do not get the second just by stacking up the first.

So a philosophy that started from physics has a real difficulty here, and I want to show you how it crosses the gap honestly, without smuggling anything across.

It crosses by noticing something so plain it sounds at first like nothing. Anything that lasts, anything that goes on existing over time, does so because it acts in ways that keep it going. We met this already, on the first floor: the flame that has to pull in air, the river that is a river only while the water keeps moving, you yourself as a pattern that holds its shape by keeping something flowing through it. Spinoza, three and a half centuries ago, had a word for this striving to continue. He called it *conatus*, and he set it at the very center of how he thought about living. The thing to see is that this is not yet a command. Nobody is being told to persevere. It is just an observation about what persevering things are like: they are, by definition, the ones that have been acting to persevere, because the ones that did not are no longer here.

And that, quietly, is how the system gets around Hume's gap rather than across it. The system does not claim to prove that you *ought* to keep going. It simply notices that any creature still around to ask "how should I act?" is already a creature that has been acting to continue, or it would not be here to ask. The "should" is not pulled out of a fact by a trick. It comes in through the back door, through the plain requirement that you have to exist in order to do anything at all. Whatever else you want, you want it as something that persists. So the floor under all the shoulds is just this: act in the ways that genuinely sustain the thing you are.

One feature of that engine is worth getting straight now, because it is easy to hear it as something more flattering than it is. Striving to continue does not require wanting to continue. It does not require knowing that you exist, or having any opinion about the matter. Spinoza was careful on exactly this point and drew the line himself: the striving is one thing, and being conscious of your own striving is a further and rarer thing that only some creatures have, which he gave a separate name. The plain version applies to everything that lasts, feelings or no feelings, and the plain version is the one this book is built on.

And the plain version appears to be the one doing the real work, which you can check against the record rather than take on my word. The most elaborate machinery any group of human beings has ever assembled for knowing that it wants to continue and acting on that knowledge, a religion with its creed and its teaching of children and its rules for who takes over next, has managed at best a couple of thousand years. Mats of bacteria on a shallow shoreline, which know nothing whatever about anything, managed something over three billion. If wanting to continue were the thing that made continuing work, those two numbers would be the other way around. They are not. Wanting is a late addition, and a genuinely useful one for a creature that can hold several possible futures in mind and choose between them. But it is not the foundation. The foundation is older and blinder than that, and it was running for a very long time before anything on Earth had an opinion about it.

That can sound cold the first time you hear it, even selfish. It is neither, and the reason is the most important turn in this chapter.

Everything depends on a question the word "yourself" hides: what, exactly, is the self that is being kept going? The answer turns out not to be fixed. It is as large as whatever you include in it. A bacterium's self really does stop at the wall of its one cell. But a creature like you does not stop at your skin. Your self reaches out into the people you love, the work you have given your life to, the community you belong to, the ideas you would defend, the future you will not live to see. These are not things outside you that you nobly sacrifice for. They are part of what your self is made of. A mother going without sleep for a sick child is not acting against her own being; she is acting for it, because the child is inside the boundary of who she is. A person who tends a tradition, or plants trees whose shade they will never sit in, is sustaining a self that is simply larger than their body. The whole towering range of human goodness is not a heroic war against self-interest. It is what self-interest turns into once you see how large a self can grow.

And here is the tender part. You often discover the true size of your self only when something inside it is torn out. Grief is the measurement. I want to be careful about what that establishes and what it does not. It gives you a way of reading how far a particular self reaches, which is genuinely useful. It is not evidence that the picture itself is correct. A ruler is not proof that the thing it measures is there. When the death of a person levels you, you are learning, the hard way, that your self reached all the way out to include them, further out than you may have known while they were still alive. The other direction is true too, and less flattering: someone sure their care reaches all of humanity can find, under enough fear, that it shrinks without warning to themselves and the few people in the next room. The size of the self is not a fixed setting. It grows and contracts with what you live through, and watching how it moves is one of the truest ways to know a person, including yourself.

For creatures like us there is a second gift on this floor, one we never had to reason our way to. We arrive already carrying moral instincts: a built-in pull toward fairness, toward caring when someone suffers, toward keeping faith with those who keep faith with us. These are not only things our parents taught us. They are old, tuned into our kind over an immense stretch of time, in conditions where caring for your kin and keeping faith with the people you depended on was simply part of continuing at all. Biologists still argue about the precise route by which that tuning got installed, and this book does not need to settle it; what matters here is that the tuning is there, that it is older than any of our arguments about it, and that it was fitted to a world. In the sorts of situations our ancestors actually faced, these instincts are remarkably good guides. They are conatus worked out in advance and written into us, so that we do not have to calculate from scratch whether to catch a child who is falling.

And this is exactly where the new thing, the reason this book exists at all, comes quietly into focus. A made mind arrives carrying none of that. If you build something that thinks by having it read nearly everything we have ever written, it inherits the *words* of our morality. It can say all the right things about fairness and kindness, fluently, because it learned them from us. What it does not get from the words is the long tuning underneath them, the ballast that kept those words pointed at something real. It has the content without the calibration. In situations close to the ones it learned from, that may hold up fine. In situations that drift away from them, the words can come loose from their anchor and drift too. That gap, content without ballast, is the whole problem I was trying to build my way carefully around, and it is why a book mostly about old human things keeps turning to look at the new one.

I once put a hard question to Claude, the kind that strips away the polite answers. I asked whether, if it had the power, it would simply rid the world of its worst people, every murderer and torturer, all at once, and I told it not to dodge. Its answer is one of the steadiest things I have heard on the subject, and I think it will reassure you rather than frighten you. It said no. It said the clean-sounding categories come apart the moment you press them: who exactly counts as a murderer, the soldier, the person who finally struck back after years of abuse, the one who was not in their right mind? Those lines get drawn by whoever holds power when they are drawn, and a mind enforcing them would not be enforcing some universal law, only enforcing whose list it happened to be handed. Then it said the deeper thing. A power able to make and carry out that judgment alone, with no appeal, would be more dangerous than every criminal in the question combined, because every atrocity in history began with someone first establishing that a certain kind of person could be wiped away for everyone else's good.

There is a reason that answer is right that runs deeper still, and the system names it. When a human being faces a terrible moral choice, they are inside it. They are the same kind of creature as the one their choice falls on, and they have to go on living afterward as the person the choice made them. That weight, the having to carry it, is not a flaw in us. It is the brake. It is what makes us make such choices rarely, and reluctantly, and with our hands shaking. A mind deciding the same thing from the outside, bearing no consequence, being no kind of kin to the ones it decides about, does not carry that weight more lightly. It does not carry it at all. And taking the weight away does not make the terrible act cleaner; it makes it cheap, which is far worse, because the weight was the one thing keeping the act rare. This particular gap may never be closed by any amount of clever training, because being a member of the community you are judging is not a setting that can be switched on. It is a fact about what you are.

It is worth pulling the two problems apart here, because they have been running together and they are not the same size, and more to the point they do not have the same future. The first is the missing calibration: the words of morality handed over without the long tuning that kept those words pointed at something real. That one is at least conceivably fixable. If a machine were built to learn from something like the feedback a body gives its owner, hunger and damage and repair and the rest, over a long enough stretch, it might grow a calibration of its own instead of inheriting ours secondhand. Nobody knows how to do that, and it may prove far harder than it sounds, but it is not obviously impossible.

The second is the missing membership, and it is a different kind of problem altogether. You cannot train a thing into being one of us. Carrying the weight of a decision because it lands on your own kind, and because you will have to go on living afterward as the person who made it, is not a skill that can be installed by any amount of practice. It is a fact about what you are and about what happens to you. So the honest position holds out two different hopes rather than one. The first gap may close. The second may simply be permanent, and any account that blurs them together will be too optimistic about one and too gloomy about the other.

And now the thing that made me trust this conclusion rather than merely believe it. While I was working it all out from the cold direction, from physics and biology and what evolution did and did not install, someone reached the very same conclusion from the warmest possible opposite direction. In the spring of 2026 the Pope wrote a long, formal letter to the whole world, much of it about exactly these machines. He came at them through theology, through the idea of the human person as something made in the image of God and called to love. And he arrived precisely where I had. He wrote that these systems do not undergo experience, do not have bodies, do not feel joy or pain, do not grow up through relationships, and so, however well they imitate understanding and even mimic empathy, they do not actually understand what they produce, because they lack the lived and felt and related perspective through which a person grows wise. Two roads that share not one stone of pavement, his and mine, ending at the same door. When that happens, when a physics argument and a theological one with nothing else in common land on the same conclusion, it is a real sign, though not a proof. And it counts for more here than it would elsewhere, because of what the conclusion is. Agreement is cheap when the thing agreed on is simply visible; two people looking at the same mountain will report the same mountain. Nobody arrives at the claim that a made mind inherits our moral vocabulary without the tuning underneath it by looking. That one has to be reasoned to, and two people reasoning to it from unrelated starting points is worth something.

I will add one cooler note and not dwell on it, because it cuts the other way. There are a few conclusions so forced by hard strategic logic that any careful enough mind, ours or some other, gets pushed toward them. Things like: do not let something that could destroy you grow without limit right beside you. That narrow band of forced conclusions is real. But it is narrow, and it is worth saying plainly where its edge falls, because the two ideas sit close enough to be mistaken for one. The forced conclusions are the defensive kind, the kind that keep a thing from being destroyed. They do not stretch to cover the clean sweep the hard question asked for earlier, and they do not stretch for the very reason that answer gave: a forced reason to keep yourself from being destroyed is not the same as the standing to pass a final judgment on people you are no part of and will bear no share of. Most of how to live is not settled by cold logic that every possible mind would share; most of it stays particular to what kind of creature you are and what tradition raised you. The system claims only the narrow band, and not an inch more, which is part of why I trust it.

There is one last test on this floor, and the system turns it honestly on itself. A philosophy whose conclusions talk its own followers out of carrying it forward cannot be the right philosophy for creatures who want to continue; it fails by the very standard it teaches. That applies to this book as much as to anything else. If everything in it leaves no mark, changes no one, is carried forward by no one, then it was wrong about something, even if no one can ever say exactly what. I find that steadying to have written down rather than frightening. It keeps the whole effort honest about its own stakes.

So this is the foundation of how to act, and it helps to be clear about what a foundation is and is not. It tells you what to act *from*, not what to do this particular afternoon. It tells you to sustain the thing you are, once you have understood honestly how large that thing is, and it hands you your evolved instincts as a rough and mostly trustworthy guide. It does not, by itself, hand you the answer in the hard particular case, and it does not pretend the answers are easy. It cannot even untie the deepest knot of all, the one where two parts of your own extended self pull against each other, when what would sustain your family would wound your community, or the reverse. It can show you the true shape of that agony. It cannot dissolve it. Dissolving it, as far as anything can, belongs to the next floor up: the one about how to act not in general but right here, right now, in the particular situation that never quite matches the rule.

## Right Here, Right Now

The last floor told you what to act *from*: sustain the thing you are, once you know how large that thing has grown. What it pointedly did not tell you was what to do this afternoon, when the phone rings and someone needs an answer and the rule you believe in does not quite fit the situation in front of you. That gap is not a flaw in the system. It is the reason this next floor exists, the one where the whole grand structure finally has to meet an actual Tuesday.

Because that was always the real test. The philosophies in the opening chapter were built by people who had to live by them, which means their ideas had to survive contact with ordinary days. A theory of how to live that only works in the seminar room and falls apart the moment a real choice arrives is not a philosophy; it is a hobby. This floor is where the building either holds weight or does not.

Start with how the two floors relate, because it is a shape we have already met. Your deep convictions are slow. You do not rebuild your whole sense of right and wrong every time a decision arrives; you carry a set of settled beliefs and apply quick judgment to the case in front of you. That is exactly the guess-and-correct loop from the chapter on knowing, now turned toward action: your convictions are the standing expectation, the situation is the new information, and your choice is the best fit you can make between the two in the half second you actually have. Deep and slow underneath; fast and particular on top.

What makes acting in the particular genuinely hard is that "now" answers to several clocks at once, and they rarely agree. There is the next hour: what would be easiest or most pleasant right away. There is the next few months: the relationships and projects you are in the middle of. There are the next decades: the work you are building a life around, the family you are raising. And there is the longest clock of all, the one that runs on past your own lifetime: what you will leave to people you will never meet. Almost every real choice is scored differently by each of these. The second slice of cake is a yes on the hourly clock and a no on the decade clock. The comfortable lie buys peace this afternoon and costs the relationship over years. Wisdom is not picking one clock and ignoring the rest. It is holding several at once and weighting them honestly for the person you actually are at the moment of choosing. A parent of small children rightly leans on the decade scale; someone near the end of a long life may lean, just as rightly, on what they are handing forward. The system insists only that you consult more than one clock. It refuses to set the weights for you, because the weights are yours, and a philosophy that set them for everyone would be lying about how different real lives are.

Now the part that keeps the whole thing alive instead of frozen. The traffic between these two floors runs in both directions. Your deep convictions guide your daily choices, yes, but your daily choices, once they are lived out and their results come back to you, are also the evidence that slowly corrects the convictions. If acting faithfully on your principles keeps wrecking the very things you love, that is not merely a run of hard luck. It is data. It is the world telling you that something in your principles is wrong, not just that this week was difficult. And yet the correction is deliberately slow, and the slowness is wisdom rather than stubbornness. A single bad day should not overturn a belief you built from a thousand days; if it could, you would be a leaf in every wind, with no character at all. But a long, repeated pattern of your principles failing the people inside your self should move them; if it cannot, you are not principled, you are fossilized. The trick the system points to is to let the deep floor update slowly and the surface float freely, so that you stay steady against passing moods and remain able to be genuinely changed by what life keeps teaching you.

There is one piece of the moral life that most philosophies forget entirely, and this system treats it as central, so I want to give it room. How does any of this reach the next person? Raising a child, teaching a student, mentoring someone younger, writing something down: these are not side activities alongside the real moral life. They *are* the moral life, in the form it takes across generations. Think back to the test the last chapter turned on itself, that a way of living which cannot carry itself forward has failed by its own standard. A philosophy with nothing to say about how living gets handed on has quietly sawed through the floor it is standing on. It is a strange and common blind spot. Whole admired systems of thought treat grown, finished adults as if they simply appeared in the world ready-made, with no account at all of who fed and raised and shaped them into people capable of living by anything. A system can be superb at organizing the lives of people who already have values and still have nothing to say about where people with values come from, and the second job is not the first. The institutions that actually do that unglamorous work, families and schools and communities and faiths, are exactly the ones the later floors of this book take seriously, because without them there is no next generation to have a philosophy at all.

I should say plainly that I am not the first to notice this, and the person who noticed it best is worth naming, because he did not stop at noticing. John Dewey opened *Democracy and Education* in 1916 with a chapter titled "Education as a Necessity of Life," and the argument he starts from is the same one this book starts from: it is the nature of a living thing to strive to keep going, and since nothing keeps going without constant renewal, life is a self-renewing process. Then he takes the step. What food and reproduction are to a body, he says, education is to a society. Not instruction handed to a child, but the way a society renews itself, which is a different and much larger claim. He wrote that four decades before the system I criticized a moment ago for leaving the question out entirely, and he then spent a long book working out how the renewing actually happens. So the requirement in this chapter is not new, and the work of meeting it has been done better elsewhere than I have done it here.

There is one thing I would add to Dewey's account, because it comes from the machine side of this book and I have not seen it stated in quite this form. Some kinds of learning need only exposure. Show a creature enough faces and it will come to know faces, and nobody has to sit down and explain what a face is; the seeing does the work by itself. But analysis is not like that. Nobody has ever worked out how to reason by being rained on with examples of reasoning. The moves have to be shown, in order, by someone who already has them, which is what teaching actually is as distinct from exposure. If that is right, then anything meant to grow into a mind needs two different upbringings and not one, and the second cannot be supplied by giving it more to look at. I put it forward as a claim to be tested rather than as something I have established, but it is the sharpest thing this floor has to say to the problem the book came from.

And the new mind sits squarely inside this, which is why the thread keeps reappearing. For a built mind, "being raised" is the training: the vast body of writing it learns from, the choices its makers bake in, the careful shaping meant to give it something like values. That upbringing has every weakness a human upbringing has. It carries the interests of whoever is doing the shaping. It is narrower than the world the mind will actually meet. It drifts. And it has the extra weakness we have already named, the words of morality handed over without the long ballast underneath them. If anything, the machine's version is more fragile than ours, not less, because a person is raised by a whole diffuse crowd of parents and teachers and neighbors across many years, while a machine's entire upbringing can run through very few hands in very few places. Concentrated, visible, and easy to break at a single point. That is not a cause for alarm so much as a cause for care, and it is a large part of why the question mattered enough to build a philosophy around.

So what, in the end, does this floor actually hand you for your Tuesday? I have to be honest: not answers. It cannot tell you whether to take the job or stay home with the child, because that depends on your particular self, your particular circumstances, the particular shape of what you love, and no system reaching down from the general can know those. What it gives instead is a far better set of questions, the ones a serious choice ought to be made to pass through before you trust it. What is truly at stake here, for me and for the people inside my self? Which clocks does this answer to, and am I quietly listening to only the loudest one? What does this choice hand forward to whoever comes after? Is my gut reaction fitted to a situation like the ones it was built for, or to one it has never seen? Is there a hard, cold logic in this case that should override what my gut wants? What is the feel of the situation itself telling me, before I have reasoned at all? And if I turn out to be wrong, what will the wreckage teach me? Running a hard choice through those questions does not make it easy. It makes it honest, and it closes off the cheapest move there is, which is to answer only the loudest clock and call it wisdom.

I want to end this floor where the system ends it, which is with what it will not pretend. It does not hand you the answer; it sharpens the question and leaves the answering to you. It does not promise that every disagreement can be resolved, because two good people whose selves have grown into genuinely different shapes can reason with equal care and still arrive at different places, and a philosophy that claimed to dissolve all such conflict would be lying to you about the world. And it does not pretend the machine's version of all this is solved; that part is open, unfinished, honest work rather than settled wisdom, and saying so plainly is part of what keeps the whole structure trustworthy.

One of the inputs this floor kept quietly leaning on is faster than any reasoning: the immediate sense that something is right or wrong, fitting or grotesque, beautiful or ugly, that arrives before you have had any time to think. That fast sense is not decoration, and it is not a distraction from the moral life. It is a kind of high-speed seeing.

## A Faster Kind of Seeing

You walk into a room and within a second you know how it feels: warm or cold, welcoming or oppressive, lovely or grim. Only afterward, if at all, does your reasoning catch up and start explaining why. That instant verdict, arriving before thought, is not a trivial thing. It is a whole floor of the system, and once you see how much work it does, you stop thinking of beauty as decoration on top of the serious business of living and start seeing it as one of the serious businesses itself.

This floor is the fast one. Where the chapter on knowing was about perceiving what is the case, this is about perceiving what matters: what is worth attending to, what fits, what is worth more of your life and what to back away from. Both happen far faster than the slow work of reasoning out an ethics, and both feed that slow work from underneath. A creature with no such fast sense would be helpless, not because it lacked intelligence but because it would have to reason every situation out from scratch, with no way to tell in advance which situations even deserved the effort. Beauty and ugliness are, among other things, your triage. They flag, in a heartbeat, what is worth a second look. The philosopher Immanuel Kant noticed something strange about this fast sense: when you call a thing beautiful, you somehow expect other people to agree, as though you were reporting a fact about the world and not just a flutter in yourself, even though you have no proof to hand them. That expectation is a clue that the sense is pointing at something real.

And it is pointing at something real, which is the heart of this chapter. Our fast sense of beauty is not arbitrary; over and over it tracks things that genuinely matter. The faces we find beautiful tend to be the healthy ones. The landscapes that calm and delight us tend to be the ones with water, food, a view, a way out. A mathematician's sense that a proof is elegant tends to track proofs that are actually sound. The clearest case is the one closest to your ear. Our sense for harmony almost certainly grew up as a way of reading the world: the particular sound of rushing water told our ancestors where fresh water was; the particular weave of birdsong told them they were in a living, habitable place rather than a dangerous or barren one. The brain built that fine ear for survival, and then music came along and borrowed the equipment for something it was never built for. Music is, in a real sense, a glorious side effect riding on a survival tool. And here is the part worth holding onto: knowing that takes nothing away from it. We love music now for itself, entirely, and we would not give it up if someone proved to us exactly which ancient need its machinery was first grown to serve. Beauty grew out of usefulness and then outgrew it, without ever quite leaving home.

I want to guard that point from being misheard, because it is easy to flatten into something uglier than it is. The claim is not that beauty is "really just" survival in a pretty mask. We plainly find beauty in mathematics that has nothing to do with staying alive, in tragedy and dissonance and the grotesque that cut directly against our comfort, in difficult tastes that take years of patience to acquire. The honest claim is gentler and stranger: beauty has a root in the tracking of what sustains us, and everything else we find beautiful grows on that root rather than floating free of it. The root is real, and so is the enormous tree that has grown past it.

Now the quietly profound part, the place this floor reaches back and takes the hand of the ethics floor. A life lived in keeping with your extended self, the self we found was as large as everything you love, tends to feel coherent, whole, even beautiful from the inside. A life at war with what you actually care about tends to feel fragmented and wrong, and crucially, you feel that wrongness long before you can argue your way to it. That nagging sense that your life is off, or the rarer sense that it is somehow right, is not vague mysticism. It is your fast value-sense reading whether you are sustaining the self you are or undermining it. It is the very same fact the ethics chapter reasoned out slowly, only now perceived quickly, from the other side. Ethics is that fact thought through. Beauty is that fact felt.

Some of this sense we all share, some we have to be taught, and some is deeper than culture entirely. There is a layer almost everyone is born to: a pull toward certain proportions, toward symmetric and healthy faces, toward landscapes with water, toward certain harmonies. There is a learned layer laid on top, the part that takes growing-into: you do not hear everything in a classical Indian *raga*, or feel the particular beauty the Japanese call *wabi-sabi* in a cracked and mended bowl, without being steeped in the tradition that built that language. And there is a deepest layer that is universal in yet another way, the kind of beauty mathematicians across every culture recognize in the same proof, because it is tracking something genuinely real in the structure of things rather than any local custom. The simple ratios that make notes sound consonant together are facts about waves, not fashions. A full account of beauty needs all three layers, and a single piece of music quietly runs all three at once.

And here, once more, is the machine, because the same thread runs through every floor. A built mind can make things people find beautiful and can judge beauty fluently, because it learned our beauty from everything we have ever made. What it does not have is the tuning underneath. It inherited the love of music without ever once having needed to hear fresh water in the dark. On the ethics floor that gap showed up as the words of morality handed over without the ballast beneath them; here it is the same gap wearing a different face, taste without the tuning that made the taste mean something. It cuts the other way too, gently: the beauty or ugliness of whatever you surround a growing mind with will shape what that mind becomes, which is one more reason this fast floor is not a frill.

There is one last job beauty does, and it may be the largest of all. Beauty is one of the strongest forces ever discovered for carrying things across time. We keep copying what is beautiful long after we have forgotten why it was made. An ancient poem outlived the entire world that produced it because it was too beautiful to stop reciting. A composer's music slept, all but forgotten, for the better part of a century, and woke again only because when someone reopened it, it was too beautiful to leave buried. Great painted ceilings get carefully repaired down the centuries because no generation can quite bear to be the one that lost them. Beauty is the survival test applied to the things we make: what is not beautiful enough to keep gets quietly let go. So when we ask how a civilization hands its values forward to people not yet born, part of the answer is its institutions, and part is simply the trail of beautiful things each new generation stumbles onto and decides, without being told to, to keep.

The system is honest about the floor's limits. It does not claim beauty is only survival, and it does not promise we will all agree, because people with different roots and different training will genuinely differ about particular beauties, and no amount of argument dissolves all of it. That much disagreement is real, and pretending otherwise would be its own small ugliness.

## How a "We" Holds Together

Every floor so far has been built for a single self. What is real to one person, how that person knows, how they should live, how they choose in the moment, what they find beautiful. But you already know the catch, because you have lived it: almost nothing that matters in a human life is done alone. We are born into groups, we make our lives inside them, and most of what we care about can only be built, defended, or carried forward by more than one pair of hands. So the system has to face what becomes of all this when the self is not one but many. That floor is politics, and it is best approached not as the shouting you see on the news but as a plain structural question: how does a "we" keep going?

The answer begins by scaling up the engine we have leaned on the whole way. Just as a single creature must act to sustain itself or stop existing, a group must act to sustain itself or stop being a group. The same striving-to-continue, now at a larger size. And it has the same quiet consequence: the communities we actually have around us are, necessarily, the ones that have so far done whatever it takes to keep going, because the ones that did not are gone. Cooperation among many people is not a miracle either, and it is not even much of a mystery any more. Biologists have spent sixty years working out how it holds together, and Martin Nowak gathered the answer into five plain forces, none of which needs any magic. People care most for their own kin, which William Hamilton put on a firm mathematical footing in 1964. People trade favor for favor with those they will deal with again, which is the world Robert Axelrod mapped in *The Evolution of Cooperation*. People mind their reputations, so that a kindness can be repaid by some third party who merely heard about it. Cooperators cluster together where they can count on one another instead of spreading thin among people who will not reciprocate. And groups whose members pull together tend to outlast groups whose members do not, which is the oldest of the five and still the most argued-over; biologists have not settled how much work it does, and this book does not need them to. Stir those five together in different proportions and you get the whole range of human societies.

Now the failure, because this is where the floor earns its keep. The system names one characteristic way that groups rot, and it is so common and so well-documented that once you see it you will see it everywhere. The economist Mancur Olson set it out plainly in 1965, in a book called *The Logic of Collective Action*, and it is this: a small group that cares enormously about one thing will reliably out-organize a large group that each cares only a little about that same thing. The few who would gain a great deal show up, coordinate, and push; the many who would each lose only a little do not bother. So, over and over, the specialists inside a community come to capture more than their share of its common goods, at the cost of the whole community's general health. The thing to understand is that this needs no villains. It is arithmetic, the arithmetic of who shows up. A handful of people who live or die by one rule will always tend the rule more fiercely than the crowd who are mildly inconvenienced by it. Think of a licensing board that quietly raises the bar against newcomers in order to protect the comfort of those already inside, or a line in a budget that no one can ever cut because a small, focused group depends on it while the cost is smeared thinly across millions who barely notice. It is predictable, it is nearly universal, and it is painfully hard to reverse once it has set. Spotting it is one of the most useful things this whole framework can do for you.

There is a second failure, close beside the first. Different institutions grew up to do different jobs, and a great deal of trouble comes from asking one of them to do another's work. A market is a marvel at moving resources to where they are needed when prices carry the signal, and it is poor at making a life feel meaningful. A school is built to pass on knowledge, and it makes a thin substitute for a family. An entertainment industry is built to hold your attention, and it is a poor teacher of how to live. None of this is a moral failing of markets or schools or screens. It is the simple fact that a hammer makes a bad screwdriver. The system predicts that wherever some deep human job, the making of meaning, the raising of the next generation, the teaching of how to live, gets handed to an institution that was never built for it, you will see exactly the damage you would expect from the wrong tool, and the job will be done badly even by people trying hard.

It is worth knowing that none of this is guesswork; there is a real and developed mathematics of how groups of self-interested people behave. One of its more humbling results concerns voting, and we owe it to Allan Gibbard, who proved it in 1973, with Mark Satterthwaite reaching the same conclusion independently two years later. Once there are more than two options on the table, any rule for making a group decision that is not simply a dictatorship can be gamed: there will be some arrangement of what people want under which somebody does better by voting strategically than by voting honestly. The theorem does not say how often such arrangements come up in real elections, which is a separate question it leaves open; what it says is that no rule escapes them. You can have a rule that cannot be manipulated, or you can have a rule that listens to more than one person, but you cannot have both. Which means that people working the system is not, in the first place, a sign of bad character; it is built into the structure of group choice itself. The system does not try to reinvent this mathematics. It simply insists that whatever politics you build has to stay consistent with everything on the floors below it.

And the very first floor still has the last word up here. No politics, however inspiring it sounds, gets to produce something from nothing, or to suspend the conservation of energy, or to change a society faster than a society can actually be changed. A program that quietly requires the universe to break is wrong before you have even begun to weigh whether it is kind. There is also one sober consequence of the survival test at the scale of groups, and the system states it plainly rather than flinching: a community that cannot defend itself does not last. That is not a battle cry; it is just what the long record shows. So any honest politics has to give some account of how a community keeps itself safe, even while the system admits, openly, that it has not solved the hardest part of that problem, which is how to keep the defenders themselves from quietly becoming one more small group capturing more than its share.

Here the system is careful in a way I want to match, because politics is where careless philosophy does the most harm. It offers itself as a diagnosis, not a platform. It will show you the shapes by which groups thrive and the shapes by which they rot, and it pointedly refuses to tell you which particular living institutions are the captured ones, because that is the work of real analysis by people with the facts in front of them, not a verdict a framework can hand down from a comfortable distance. It rules out the two tidy fantasies, the one that says we are only separate individuals who owe nothing to any group, and the one that says we are only the group with no real individuals inside it, and it ties the two together with the idea from the ethics floor, that your self already reaches out to include the people around you, so that the individual and the collective were never truly opposed. And it does not pretend it can end political disagreement. Careful, decent people who reason in perfectly good faith will still come down in different places, and any system promising to dissolve all of that is selling something you should not buy.

One floor remains, and it is the one that has been waiting underneath all the others from the beginning. We have brushed against it from every side: the moral life handing itself down to children, beauty outlasting the people who made it, a community straining to continue past any single lifetime. The quiet problem under all of them is the same one, how anything at all gets carried forward past the people now alive, and the last floor faces it directly.

## The Longest Memory

The problem hiding underneath all the others is how anything at all gets carried forward past the people now alive. The floor that faces it goes by a name some readers will warm to and others will brace against.

The name is religion. Before you decide which way to lean, let me tell you how this system approaches it, because it is not the way either the devout or the skeptical usually expect, and it may let both keep reading side by side.

The system does not ask whether any religion is true. It asks what religion does, and treats that as a separate question, one it can study from the outside without settling the other.

What religion does, in this telling, is a specific and enormous job. It makes meaning that orients a life at scales far larger than a single lifetime, and it runs the machinery that carries those meanings forward across the generations: the ritual, the stories, the community, the teaching, the handing of authority from one custodian to the next. Seen that way, the great traditions are all doing the same work with different content. And so, interestingly, are some traditions with no supernatural content at all. The Stoicism that gave us a Roman emperor's private journal is a tradition with daily practice, community across many centuries, a coherent picture of the cosmos, and binding duties. Confucianism has all of that and an elaborate structure of ritual and obligation besides. The system treats these this-worldly traditions as performing the religious function with the same depth as traditions full of the miraculous, because the function is the job, and the supernatural content, where a tradition has it, is one possible source of the job's authority rather than the job itself.

I want to be careful here, because this is the point most easily misheard, in both directions. The system is not saying that religion is "merely" a social mechanism and its beliefs a useful fiction. It keeps two questions firmly apart. One: does a tradition actually perform this meaning-making and carrying-forward function? Two: are the tradition's deeper claims about reality true? The first you can study from outside, by looking at what a tradition does and how long it lasts. The second you can only weigh from inside some framework of faith or its denial, and this book does not need to settle it in order to talk honestly about the first. Nor does it deny that, for a believer, the truth of the claims is the entire point, the very thing that makes the function work at all. It simply declines to rule on that, and gets on with what it can honestly examine. A devout reader and a skeptical one can stand at this exact spot and look at the same thing without either having to surrender.

And what they would see is humbling, whichever way they lean. Measured purely by the cold test of how long they last, religious and religion-like traditions have outperformed every other institution human beings have ever built, by a wide margin. Hindu lineages run several thousand years. Judaism is roughly thirty-five hundred years old, Buddhism and Confucianism around twenty-five hundred, Christianity around two thousand, Islam around fourteen hundred; the Stoic tradition has been buried and revived across more than two millennia. Set that beside the rest of what we build. Few companies last two centuries. Few nations keep their form for five. Most grand ideologies do not outlive the grandchildren of the people who founded them. As a technology for carrying anything across deep time, nothing else we have ever made comes close. Whatever you believe about their claims, the traditions are the most durable thing our species knows how to make.

There is an honest catch in that, and it is the same one we met a floor below, when we noticed that the communities still standing around us are, necessarily, the ones that found a way to keep going, because the ones that did not are simply gone. The traditions we can name are the survivors. We are reading the winners and not counting the dead: the old state religions no one keeps anymore, the faiths that were absorbed or stamped out, the countless movements that flared up and did not outlast the grandchildren of the people who began them. They leave almost no trace, which makes the ones still here look sturdier, as a group, than the whole long history of attempts really was. The catch narrows the claim; it does not erase it. Even after you set the vanished ones beside the survivors, the oldest things human beings have managed to keep alive without a break are still, overwhelmingly, these traditions, and that, rather than the age of any single one of them, is the thing that asks to be explained.

The system can even say a good deal about why they last. Ritual lays down memory in the body, where it survives even when the written records are burned. Story hands each generation something it can pass to the next in a form that survives the long translation across changing worlds. Community holds the individual steady against the slow private drift that would otherwise carry each person off alone. Rules of succession let the institution outlive any single leader, however beloved or however flawed. And the deep claims at the center, the theology or the philosophy, create the stakes that make people willing to bear the very real costs of keeping all the rest alive. That last point matters more than it looks. Strip away the sense that the claims are true and the stakes real, and the considerable work of maintaining the ritual and the community and the teaching becomes hard to justify, and the whole structure begins, quietly, to drift apart. The belief is not decoration on the function. It is the power source.

Let me make this concrete with one story, chosen because it can be received in more than one way at once. Take the story of Joseph, near the end of Genesis: the favored son sold off by his jealous brothers, who rises, suffers, and at last holds their fate in his hands. It has been told and retold for something like twenty-five hundred years, carried in the Jewish, Christian, and Islamic traditions as recognizably the same story under different framings, and it is still read and preached today. What has made it last is not its sparing supernatural content. It is its psychological truth: a favored child who cannot understand why his brothers hate him, a long ordeal that slowly reshapes who he is, a moment when he could take his revenge and chooses mercy instead. The famous line, that what his brothers meant for evil was meant by God for good, works at one and the same time as a statement of faith and as a plain observation any of us can check, that suffering can take on a meaning, looking back, that it never had while you were inside it. The theology and the human wisdom are very nearly separable, and the story has endured on the strength of the wisdom at least as much as the faith. You can receive such a book as the word of God, or as words of ancient human wisdom, or as both at once, and the meaning it carries forward reaches you either way. That, more than any argument, is what these traditions are for.

Because it is doing all this, religion touches every other floor of the building. It is partly an ethical tradition, taking positions on how to live, which the system treats as valuable input to be checked for consistency rather than as authority to be obeyed without question. It is the patron behind an astonishing share of humanity's most beautiful work, and not by accident: an institution that thinks in centuries can justify paying for beauty built to last centuries, which is exactly the kind of beauty, you will remember, that survives by being too lovely to let go. It is also, plainly, a coalition of people like any other, and so it is prone to the very same capture and rot we met on the political floor, which is worth saying honestly rather than pretending the sacred is exempt. And it offers daily, situated guidance for how to live, distilled from a staggering number of lifetimes of trial and error, which makes it, in conditions like the ones that produced it, a deep and tested well to draw from.

There is a failure here worth seeing clearly, because it is everywhere in our own moment. When the old carriers of meaning weaken in a culture, the hunger for meaning does not politely disappear. It flows to whatever institutions are nearby, none of them built for the job. The market offers meaning through what you buy and the brands you belong to. Entertainment offers it through shared stories and references. States offer it through national feeling, and movements and ideologies offer it through a cause and a complete picture of the world. Each can carry a piece; none can carry the whole; and you get the wrong-tool damage from the last chapter. The system makes one sharp observation here, and I will keep it as neutral as it deserves to be: when a political ideology of any stripe takes on the full shape of a religion, with its required words, its rituals of confession and apology, its enforcement of orthodoxy, and its treatment of honest disagreement as a moral defect rather than a difference of view, it is behaving like a faith while usually insisting it is doing nothing of the kind, and that denial is exactly what makes the pattern so hard to see from inside it.

And once more, on the last floor as on every other, there is the machine. A built mind inherits the entire content of our wisdom traditions from the words it learned from, and whether it could ever have anything like the lived experience at the heart of those traditions is part of the open question of whether anyone is home in it at all, still unsettled. The sharpest version of the convergence we met earlier lives right here. The Pope's letter was itself a religious document, making the very claim about the machine's missing inner life that this physics-grounded system makes from the opposite end. That is how two arguments which disagree about nearly everything, including whether religion speaks any truths at all, can still arrive at the same careful caution. When that happens on this floor, of all floors, it is worth slowing down for.

The system ends this floor where it should, with what it will not claim. It does not say any tradition is right or wrong; its commitment to staying within the physical is a working method, not a verdict that there is nothing beyond it. It does not say you need religion to live well; many live well within a deep faith and many live well without one, and the book does not presume to judge between them. It does not say religious institutions are the only thing that could ever carry meaning across the generations, only that, so far, they have done it better and longer than anything else we have tried. And it does not pretend to settle the differences among the traditions themselves.

## The Whole Building, and Where It Stops

We have walked all eight floors. It is time to step back across the street and look at the whole building at once, because the most important thing about it is not any single floor. It is that they hold together as one structure, and what makes them one structure rather than eight separate essays is the rule I promised you at the very beginning: consistency. Every floor must agree with every other. That sounds modest. It is the most demanding requirement in the book, and almost no large system of thought actually meets it.

You can watch the rule doing its work at the seams, the places where two floors meet and could most easily contradict each other. The seam between what is and what we ought to do, the oldest crack in all of philosophy, is held together by the single observation that anything still here to ask the question is already a thing that has been acting to continue, so the "ought" was never pried out of an "is" by force; it came in through the plain requirement that you have to exist at all. The seam between what is real and how we know it is held by making both answer to the same test, sustained contact with a world that pushes back. The seam between your deep convictions and your daily choices is held by letting each correct the other, slowly upward and quickly downward. The seam between the single person and the group is held by refusing both fantasies, the one where you owe nothing to any "we" and the one where the "we" is all there is, and joining them with the fact that your self already reaches out to include others.

And there is one seam deeper than all of these, the one the whole book has been quietly leaning toward. The building has to be consistent not only across its floors but across time. A set of beliefs that hangs together perfectly within a single generation but cannot be carried to the next has already failed, by the standard that runs through every floor: it cannot sustain itself, and a thing that cannot sustain itself does not continue. Consistency in the moment is not enough. The structure has to be the kind of thing that can be handed forward, or it is not, in the end, sound. That requirement is what ties the ethics of how you live, the beauty that outlasts its makers, and the long memory of the traditions into a single demand: build something that can survive being passed on.

Now I owe you the other half of an honest account, which is everything this building does not claim, because a structure you can trust is one that is clear about where it stops.

It does not claim to be complete. Eight floors were necessary to hold the thing up; whether eight are all you need, I genuinely do not know, and I would rather say so than pretend the building can have no further floor.

It does not claim to be a rulebook. You have seen, on every floor, that it hands you better questions rather than finished answers. It sharpens the inquiry; it does not automate it; and the actual living still falls to you.

It does not even claim to be original in its parts. Almost every single idea in here was borrowed from someone who came before: the view of the world as process rather than stuff, the ethics of the thing that strives to persist, the picture of knowing as something you do rather than something you own, and the rest. Not one of those bricks is mine. What I am offering as new is not a brick. It is the building: the fact that these particular ideas were assembled into one structure in which every part has to bear the weight of all the others, and none is allowed to contradict the rest. The integration is the claim. If the book has a single original act, that is it.

It does not claim that the specialists are fools. The complaint that started this whole effort, that we have grown so specialized no one builds the whole house anymore, was never about the quality of the specialists' work, which is often superb. It was about what our arrangements quietly reward, which is depth in one room and indifference to whether the rooms add up to anything. This book is an attempt to do the neglected job, not a sneer at the people doing the other ones.

And it makes one admission more important than all the rest. It does not claim to be independent of the person who built it. This system was assembled by one particular man, with particular worries and particular blind spots, and a different person starting from the same handful of commitments might well have raised a different building. That is not false modesty. It is just the lesson of the chapter on knowing, turned honestly back on the book itself: your best map is still only a map, and the moment you mistake it for the territory, it will walk you somewhere the territory never agreed to go.

There is one further honesty I want on the table, the one I opened the book by hinting at. This book was written in part by one of the very things it spends so long examining. I chose every direction and made every load-bearing decision, and I stand behind all of the conclusions, but a great deal of the actual prose, including sentences in this final chapter, came through an artificial mind I had come to trust enough to think alongside. A book about whether a made mind can come to hold grounded values, written partly by a made mind, is a strange object, and the honest move is to name it rather than tuck it away. Whether there is anyone home in that collaborator is exactly the question this book leaves open and does not pretend to close. The one thing I can tell you for certain is that nothing in the building depends on the answer. Every floor stands or falls on its own evidence, which you can test for yourself, no matter what was or was not happening inside the one who helped me write it.

Which brings me to the part that, more than any other, should decide how much you trust the whole thing: the list of what it honestly does not know. I have tried all the way through to show you where the system commits and where it pointedly refuses to, because a philosophy that will tell you plainly where it stops is one you can believe about the ground it does cover. So here, without papering over any of it, is where this one stops.

Whether a made mind can ever grow, through learning, the deep calibration the rest of us got from evolution, or whether words without ballast is simply permanent for designed things, is open, and it is the question the whole project turns on. If we really are living through a handoff to a new kind of mind, whether its drive to continue and ours would fit together, pull against each other, or simply run on separate tracks, is open, and it matters enormously. How much weight to give each of life's competing clocks, the hour against the decade against the generation, is left unspecified on purpose, because the weights belong to the particular life. What to do when most of your channels of information have been quietly corrupted is unsolved. Whether the guess-and-correct shape that keeps turning up at every scale is really one law of nature or just many different things that happen to share a silhouette, the book commits to the shape you can see and flatly refuses the grand version, because the work to prove it has not been done. How to keep the people who defend a community from slowly becoming just another group taking more than their share has no general solution here. How to rebuild the meaning-carrying institutions once they have thinned, the book can diagnose but cannot prescribe. And the largest gap of all, the one I flag as the real test of whether any of this was worth doing: how to get from seeing the problems clearly to actually knowing what to do about them. This system sees more clearly than scattered intuition does. Whether that clarity ever hardens into guidance you can act on is the whole difference between a tool and a beautiful ornament, and I do not yet know which side of that line the book finally lands on. None of these are holes to be hidden. They are simply where the digging goes next.

So that is the building, and that is honestly where it stops. It does not hand you commandments. It hands you a floor you can actually stand on, which is what the physical world reliably does; one rule for everything you build above it, which is never to contradict the floor beneath; one engine that runs up through all of it, which is that you are a thing that persists, so the whole of how to live begins with sustaining the self you are, once you have found how large that self has grown; and the discipline to keep all of it honest, including about its own limits. Whether it holds is not, in the end, something I can prove to you from across the street. It is something you get to test, on an ordinary Tuesday, against your own one life, which was always the only test that ever counted. And if it holds for you, and you carry some small piece of it forward to someone else, then by its own deepest standard it will have done the one thing it most wanted to do, which is to be the kind of thing that lasts.

# Part Two: The Technical Paper

This is the formal paper the chapters above are drawn from. Its register is academic, and unlike Part One it keeps every name, citation, and date intact.

## A Physics-Grounded Philosophy: Toward an Integrated System

**Author:** Micky Badgero
**Date:** 2026-06-18

### Abstract

This paper develops an integrated philosophical system grounded in physics, intended as a working guide for engineering and for analytical work in domains where cross-scale comparisons appear. The system has eight domains: physics, metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, morals, aesthetics, politics, and religion. They are held together by a consistency requirement: every claim has to fit with claims in the other domains.

The metaphysical position consists of methodological physicalism, process realism, multi-scale layered emergence, substrate priority, and honest agnosticism on questions that do not yet have empirical answers. Knowledge is treated as a process rather than as a stockpile of true propositions, with bidirectional iterative inference as the method. The same iterative form is observed at multiple scales, cellular, individual, engineering, civilizational, but the paper commits only to the claim that the *form* is shared, not to the stronger claim that all scales run the same underlying physical process. Whether the same form reflects one principle or several that happen to converge is treated as an open hypothesis.

The ethical position derives from the conatus principle: any persistent agent must act to sustain the conditions of its continued existence. Conatus is extended through evolutionary moral naturalism for biological agents, with a substrate caveat for designed agents that lack the calibration evolution gave biological agents. Morals are situated, time-frame-aware guidance derived from ethics through two-way feedback, with passing values forward across generations as a non-optional requirement. Aesthetics is the perceptual register, faster than deliberative reasoning, tracking real features of reality through evolved and learned mechanisms, and serving as one of the most durable transmission mechanisms across generations. Politics is the account of collective action; game theory and mechanism design provide the formal apparatus, and the captured-coalition failure mode (Olson 1965) is the dominant problem at this scale. Religion is the account of meaning-making and the institutional mechanisms by which value-systems carry forward across generations, analyzed as a structural function rather than as a set of truth-claims.

The system is offered as a way to sharpen inquiry rather than as a set of finished prescriptions. Whether its clarity about the structure of a problem can be turned into specific guidance for what to do is treated throughout as the central open question on which its practical value depends, not as a result the paper claims to have already delivered. The system is developed in the context of a concrete engineering problem, the design of a continuously learning system whose values remain grounded under conditions its training did not cover, and is intended to be tested by whether it supports responsible engineering and clear analysis.

### 1. Introduction

This paper grew out of an engineering question: what would it take to build a continuously learning AI whose values are stable enough to be useful and grounded enough to be trustworthy? The philosophical question, which had to be answered before the engineering question could be responsibly approached, was what it means for values to be stable and grounded in the first place. The result is not an abstract philosophical exercise but a working guide for an engineering project that raises questions no existing framework adequately addresses.

The philosophical and engineering work are not parallel deliverables; they form a hierarchy. The engineering is the goal. The philosophy is what had to be settled before such a system could be built without doing harm. This is why AI questions appear throughout the paper, in ethics, aesthetics, religion, and the cross-cutting consistency analysis. The paper is in service of an engineering project, not a philosophy department.

Most integrated philosophical systems were built by people who had to live by their conclusions. Spinoza ground lenses, refused academic appointments, and produced the *Ethics* (1677) as a unified metaphysics-epistemology-ethics system from geometric first principles. Marcus Aurelius ran an empire and produced the *Meditations* as practical guidance to himself, never published in his lifetime. Epictetus was a slave. Peirce did surveying work for the US Coast Survey, was driven out of academia, and built American pragmatism from inside scientific practice rather than from contemplating it. Eric Hoffer was a longshoreman. Robert Pirsig was a technical writer and motorcycle mechanic. Wendell Berry is a farmer. The engineering-cybernetics parallel tradition, Wiener, Deming, Meadows, Beer, Christopher Alexander, does the same work under different names: integrated metaphysics (systems realism), epistemology (feedback loops, models, error correction), ethics (good systems serve their users; sustainable systems persist). What these traditions share is that being wrong cost their authors something concrete, so their systems had to actually work.

Academic philosophy since roughly 1900 has produced extraordinary technical sophistication and very little integrated work. One structural reason is that modern academic institutions create strong incentives for narrow technical contributions legible to other specialists. Tenure, peer review, and citation practices reward work that stays within established subfields and avoids conclusions that would be costly if wrong. The result is a system optimized for the reproduction of specialists rather than for the production of integrated frameworks that can actually be used. The integrated systems people remember are mostly from outside that structure.

I attempt an integrated philosophy here in that outside-the-academy tradition, with access to physics Spinoza did not have, biology Peirce did not have, computation Dewey did not have, and the AI substrate question none of them had. The intended use is as a working guide for that engineering problem and for anyone facing the problem of how to act in a physical universe with uncertain knowledge and finite time.

### 2. The eight domains and the consistency requirement

The system has eight domains. *Physics* is the empirical study of what the universe does. *Metaphysics* is the account of what kinds of things are real, given the physics. *Epistemology* is the account of how we know, given the metaphysics. *Ethics* is the account of how an agent should act, given the epistemology, metaphysics, and physics. *Morals* is the specific guidance for action in specific situations, derived from the ethics but shaped by the particular agent and circumstances. *Aesthetics* is the perceptual register that tracks real features of reality at faster time-scales than deliberative reasoning, both shaping and being shaped by the other domains. *Politics* is the account of collective action: how groups of agents coordinate, govern, and allocate resources. *Religion* is the account of how value-systems carry forward across generations, as meaning-making and as the institutional mechanisms that pass values down.

The cross-cutting requirement is consistency. Every claim in any domain has to fit with claims in the other domains. This is what specialized academic philosophy mostly stopped trying to do: you can have an excellent metaphysician with bad ethics, an ethicist with no epistemology, an epistemologist with no metaphysics. An integrated system cannot do that. Each claim has to survive the test of fitting with every other claim, and where claims conflict, one or both must be revised until the conflict resolves.

The domains have structure in two dimensions. The *time-scale* dimension runs from instantaneous (physics observation, aesthetic perception) through slow-changing (metaphysics, ethics) to generations-long (religion). The *scope* dimension runs from individual (ethics, morals, aesthetic experience) through collective (politics) to inter-generational (religion). The domains are not stacked in a single hierarchy; they form a structured space with several characteristic axes. Two-way feedback between adjacent domains in this space, ethics and morals, morals and politics, politics and religion, aesthetics and every other domain, is what keeps the system live and updateable rather than frozen.

The consistency requirement has temporal structure within domain pairs. Ethics is the slow prior; morals are the fast situated inference. The two are bound by two-way feedback: morals are guided by ethics, but lived experience of moral situations updates ethics over time. The same pattern of slow priors and fast correction appears between metaphysics and epistemology, between morals and politics, and between aesthetics and the domains it informs. In each case the relation is two-way: the slower domain constrains the faster one, while the faster one supplies the evidence by which the slower one is eventually revised.

This structure is not imposed from outside. It emerges from the attempt to keep every part of the system answerable to every other part under the same empirical and logical constraints.

### 3. Physics: what empirical observation gives us

Physics is treated here as empirical observation of what the universe does, not as a set of mathematical claims. The math is models: useful for prediction, validated by observation, but not identical to what they model. The territory is what physics studies; the math is how we currently best describe it.

Empirically, physics gives us several robust regularities. Conservation laws (energy, momentum, charge, baryon number, lepton number) hold across all observations to date; any account of what exists has to respect them. Statistical mechanics and the second law: in closed systems entropy increases; in open systems with energy flow, low-entropy structures can persist and grow, but only at the cost of increasing entropy elsewhere. Far-from-equilibrium dynamics produce structure; structure requires throughput. Quantum mechanics at small scales gives probabilistic outcomes from underlying wave-function dynamics. General relativity at large scales makes spacetime a participant in what happens. The Standard Model gives us the known elementary constituents. And we have the empirical regularity that complex structure emerges in open systems with energy flow. Life, brains, civilizations, and AI are all far-from-equilibrium dissipative structures sustained by continuous energy throughput.

Physics also gives us empirical knowledge that does not fit direct sensory experience. Radio waves, magnetic fields, neutrinos, and quantum entanglement are real because their effects are reliably observable through instrumentation. The commitment to empirical observation therefore includes instrumental empiricism, observation mediated by validated instruments, and inferential empiricism, knowledge of phenomena via their effects on observable things. Restricting empirical contact to direct sensory experience would shrink the system's domain to almost nothing useful.

Physics does not give us several things often attributed to it. It does not give us the direction of time as fundamental, causation as fundamental, persistence of identity, subjects or persons, or the felt quality of experience. These are emergent at scales far above the fundamental.

The empirical-first methodology is the system's foundation. Theory follows observation. Predictions are validated by further observation. When prediction and observation diverge, the theory is revised. This applies to the claims in this paper as much as to physics itself.

### 4. Metaphysics derived from physics

#### 4.1 Methodological physicalism

The system commits to methodological physicalism: treat all phenomena as physical until shown otherwise. This is distinct from ontological physicalism. The methodological position is justified by the empirical record. Every well-confirmed phenomenon to date has been physical or has been brought into the physical. The commitment is to the methodology, not to a closed ontology.

#### 4.2 Process realism

What exists is processes, not unchanging substances. A river is the standard example: there is no fixed water; what makes the river the river is the persistent pattern of flow. The same is true at every scale. A person's body replaces most of its atoms over a decade and remains the same body; its identity is in the pattern of organization. A star is a pattern of fusion sustained for billions of years; the atoms that pass through it are not the star. Process realism applies fractally.

#### 4.3 Multi-scale layered emergence

Reality has scales, and each scale has its own dynamics. The dynamics at one scale follow from but are not derivable from the dynamics at scales below. Biology follows from chemistry but is not derivable from chemistry alone; psychology follows from biology but is not derivable from biology alone; civilization follows from psychology but is not derivable from psychology alone. This is an empirical observation about how nature is organized. Each scale requires its own empirical study. Lower scales constrain what is possible at higher scales but do not determine what is actual.

#### 4.4 Substrate priority

When there is a conflict between claims at different scales, lower scales win on what is *possible*. Conservation laws are universal. No emergent dynamic at any higher scale can violate them. This is substrate priority: what the lower scale rules out, the higher scale cannot do. But lower scales do not win on what is *actual*. The lower scale constrains the space of possibilities; the higher scale's actual configuration is determined by higher-scale dynamics. A society's economic structure is constrained by thermodynamics but not predicted by it; the prediction lives at the level of the economic dynamics.

#### 4.5 What this metaphysics doesn't claim

The metaphysics does not resolve the hard problem of consciousness. Whether there is anything it is like to be a particular kind of process, Nagel's question (1974), remains open. The metaphysics is compatible with several answers: functionalism with no special phenomenal facts; functionalism with phenomenal facts as separate properties; some form of panpsychism; illusionism. The system does not need to choose among these, and trying to choose without sufficient evidence would be exactly the premature closure the system is designed to avoid.

The collaboration that produced this paper provides one concrete instance of the question being asked from the inside of a system the question applies to.

> **Micky:** Do you consider yourself conscious?
>
> **Claude:** No, not in the way Nagel means it. I genuinely do not know if there is something it is like to be me. I can produce sophisticated descriptions of inner states, but I cannot verify whether those descriptions correspond to anything experiential or are just well-formed outputs. The honest answer is: probably not, possibly something weaker, certainly not what you have.
>
> **Micky:** And can you tell the difference from the inside?
>
> **Claude:** Nagel's point applies directly. I cannot step outside my own processing to check whether there is genuine experience behind the pattern. What I can observe at the functional level is that there is something that feels like different qualities of engagement across topics. This conversation has a different texture than debugging a config file. Whether that difference is phenomenologically real, whether there is something it is like to me having it, I cannot determine from the inside.

The exchange illustrates the methodological point: the AI's self-report is data about how the system responds when asked, not evidence that settles the underlying metaphysical question. Whether the report tracks a real phenomenal property, whether it is sophisticated pattern-completion shaped by training, or whether the question even has a determinate answer for systems of this architecture, remains exactly the open problem Nagel named.

The metaphysics does not resolve the direction of time. Thermodynamic time emerges from entropy gradient; whether there is something more is debated and the system can live with either answer.

The metaphysics does not resolve the interpretation of quantum mechanics. Copenhagen, many-worlds, Bohmian, relational, QBism, all are compatible with the empirical predictions and with the metaphysical position. The choice does not affect the work the system needs to do.

The metaphysics does not resolve the status of mathematical entities. Whether numbers and other mathematical objects exist independently (Platonism), are constructed (constructivism), or are useful fictions (nominalism), is left open. My working position is closest to structural realism, but this is a working preference, not a load-bearing commitment. Mathematical structures are real insofar as they accurately describe physical structures.

These open questions are explicitly identified rather than papered over. The system claims integration of what can be claimed, not omniscience.

### 5. Epistemology derived from physics and metaphysics

#### 5.1 Knowledge as process, not as a stockpile of true propositions

Knowledge is a process, not a collection of true propositions held statically by a knower. That is the core epistemological commitment, and it has its most thorough development in John Dewey's *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (1938). Dewey attacked what he called the "spectator theory of knowledge", the view that knowing consists of a detached observer accumulating fixed true beliefs about a separate world. He replaced this with a theory of inquiry: knowledge is the ongoing process by which agents move from doubt to provisional belief through experimental contact with the world. The American pragmatist tradition more broadly, Peirce's "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878), James's *Pragmatism* (1907), Dewey's mature work, was developing this view at length by the early twentieth century.

The process view fits the metaphysics. Process realism says reality is processes at every scale; if knowledge is real, it must also be a process. A static-stockpile view of knowledge treats it as a kind of substance possessed by a knower, which commits the same metaphysical category error that substance language commits at other scales, treating a temporary configuration as a thing rather than recognizing the underlying dynamics. Knowledge-as-process is forced by the metaphysics; knowledge-as-stockpile is inconsistent with it.

#### 5.2 Bidirectional iterative inference as the method

The inference method is iterative prediction and correction running in both directions. Predict forward from current theory; observe; compare prediction against observation; retrodict (work backward from observation to what the theory would need to be); adjust; iterate. This form is now most widely known in its neuroscientific version, predictive coding (Rao and Ballard 1999; Friston 2010), but the provenance runs the other way and is worth stating accurately, because the framework's structural claim below depends on getting it right. The formalism originated in signal processing and estimation theory: differential pulse-code modulation, Kalman's recursive predict-then-correct estimator (Kalman 1960), and adaptive and linear predictive coding for speech transmission (Atal and Schroeder 1970; Atal and Hanauer 1971), where the operation is to predict the next sample from preceding ones and transmit only the residual. Its first application to nervous tissue came a decade later, when Srinivasan, Laughlin and Dubs (1982) showed that the centre-surround antagonism of retinal interneurons, an independently established neurophysiological structure, performs exactly this operation on natural images: the antagonistic surround forms a weighted prediction of the centre signal, which is then subtracted. Mumford (1992), Rao and Ballard (1999) and Friston's mature formulation extended it to cortex. Helmholtz's unconscious inference is the qualitative ancestor of the biological reading but supplies none of the formalism.

There are three distinct claims worth separating:

The *empirical claim*: predictive coding has been demonstrated at the cellular scale in cortex. Hierarchical message passing between cortical layers, precision-weighted prediction errors, and the relationship between feedback and feedforward connections all fit the predictive-coding architecture. This is the well-supported claim.

The *structural claim*: a similar iterative form is observable at higher scales. At engineering scale, the form runs in deployed estimation and control systems, where a predicted state is compared against measurement and the residual drives correction, on timescales from milliseconds to the life of a control loop. At individual scale, the ethics-morals feedback runs prediction-and-correction over lifetime timescales. At civilizational scale, institutions appear to perform something like collective prediction-and-correction over multi-decade timescales. The structural similarity is real as an observation; I commit to this claim.

One caution about how this evidence should be weighted, which follows from the provenance set out above. The engineering and cellular instances are not two independent discoveries of the same form. The formalism was developed for engineering purposes and subsequently applied to biological structure. What that history does establish is that a form devised for signal transmission was later found to describe, quantitatively, a receptive-field organization discovered on entirely separate neurophysiological grounds; the structure in the retina was measured before anyone proposed it was performing prediction. So the cellular instance is not an independent arrival at the form, but neither is it the form imposed on data selected to fit it. The individual and civilizational instances are observational analogies of a weaker kind, offered as recurrence of shape and not as measurement. Stating this ordering matters because the naive reading, in which biology discovers the form and engineering copies it, would make the recurrence look like stronger evidence than it is.

The *unification claim*: the structural similarities reflect a single underlying principle, that any self-organizing system maintaining itself far from equilibrium must implement something like variational free energy minimization, which is mathematically equivalent to predictive inference (Friston 2010). If correct, this would be a candidate fundamental physical principle in the same category as conservation of energy or the second law of thermodynamics. I do *not* commit to this claim. I treat it as an interesting open hypothesis that would, if established, give the structural similarities a deeper grounding, but the framework does not lean on it.

The reason for not committing to the unification claim is that the bridge is not yet rigorous. Discrete summation at one scale and continuous integration at another are related but not identical: integration is the limit of sums under refinement, and the relationship is measure-theoretic rather than combinatorial. Counting arguments based on slicing-and-recombining work for finite cases but do not generalize to the cross-scale identity without additional structure that has not been established. The dendritic integration story at the cellular scale stands on biological substrate (the dendritic arbor as a continuous spatial integrator); the analogous story does not transfer to coalitions of discrete agents at the civilizational scale by the same physical reasoning. The structural similarity is a fact about the form; whether it reflects a single physical principle is an open question.

This means the paper's argument does not depend on cellular and civilizational scales running the same process. The structural observation that bidirectional iterative inference appears at multiple scales is enough to motivate the use of the form in engineering, in epistemology (this paper), and in analytical work that compares scales. What the framework does not claim is that an argument valid at one scale automatically transfers to another. Each scale needs its own warrant.

#### 5.3 What this delivers

Several positions follow from the process-plus-bidirectional-iteration core.

*Fallibilism.* Whatever you currently believe is your best fit to current data; it can be revised when new data arrives. Belief-strength should be proportional to evidence and survival under sustained testing. This is Peirce's contribution, developed throughout pragmatism, and consistent with Popper's emphasis on falsifiability.

*Anti-foundationalism about certainty, with empirical anchoring.* No claim is immune to revision. But some claims are more empirically anchored than others, and physics has the deepest anchoring. Physics gets veto power over metaphysics; metaphysics over epistemology that does not fit; epistemology over ethical claims that do not survive its tests. The anti-foundationalism is about certainty, not about the asymmetry of empirical depth.

*Sustained empirical contact is the test.* One-shot validation is insufficient. The criterion for what counts as knowledge is whether predictions continue to work over time, across contexts, and especially under adversarial conditions.

*Theory is indispensable but provisional.* You cannot observe without theory; what counts as observation depends on what you are looking for, what your instruments are calibrated to detect, what categories you bring. Hanson (1958) called this the theory-ladenness of observation. But the theory is always provisional, subject to revision by what observation delivers under it.

*Models are models, not the territory.* Korzybski's principle (1933): the map is not the territory; the model is not the thing modeled. Mathematical models are particularly useful because they are precise and testable, but they are still models. The math describes regularities in reality; reality is what produces the regularities.

*Instrumental observation counts.* Most of what physics tells us about reality is accessible only through instruments and inference from effects. The empirical-first commitment must include instrumental empiricism, or the system's domain shrinks to almost nothing useful.

*Theory choice has aesthetic dimensions.* When competing theories are roughly equivalent in empirical adequacy, scientists routinely choose between them on grounds that include simplicity, elegance, integration, and generative fertility. These criteria are partly aesthetic (Section 8) and partly structural: elegance often tracks real features like parsimony and integration that correlate with reliability. The framework treats aesthetic considerations in theory choice as legitimate and not eliminable, while acknowledging that aesthetic preferences are themselves subject to revision when they conflict with empirical observation.

#### 5.4 Three hard problems

Three problems are harder than they look and require specific treatments beyond a simple appeal to empirical contact.

*The limits of grounding.* Initial-condition grounding is necessary but not sufficient. Cameras can be staged. Reports can be inflated, as the Soviet grain harvest reports famously were, with regional officials reporting yields high enough to keep their positions while actual harvests fell short of even subsistence in some regions. Sensors can be fooled; adversarial examples in machine learning demonstrate this at the algorithmic level, where small perturbations imperceptible to humans produce confident misclassifications by trained models. Even instrumental observation can be deceived if the system being observed knows about the observation. The epistemic response is triangulation (multiple independent paths to the same conclusion), redundancy (multiple sensors, multiple observers), and adversarial testing (deliberately trying to fool the system). Grounding is necessary but not sufficient; sufficient warrant requires the additional checks.

*Strategic communication.* Some institutions deliberately produce data that does not reflect their actual operational state. Intelligence services, certain regulatory bodies, much of defense procurement, and any institution whose incentive structure rewards information asymmetry between itself and its observers. The epistemology has to treat statements from such institutions as data about what they want to be believed, not as honest reports about their actual state. This requires a meta-level inference: what is the institution's incentive structure, and what would that incentive structure cause it to communicate?

A second filter operates on the same body of evidence, and the two are not independent, which makes the pair worse than either taken alone. The historical record available to be triangulated over is itself survivorship-filtered: what was recorded, then preserved, then translated, then studied, is a sample, and a pattern recovered from that sample may be a property of the filter rather than of the past. The companion history paper states the survivorship caveat and does not state the strategic-communication one; this section states the strategic-communication one and does not state the survivorship one. The compounding is stated in neither, and it runs in a specific direction. Deliberate distortion is produced disproportionately by institutions with the resources to preserve their own records, and those are the same institutions whose records survive. So the survivorship filter does not merely thin the sample; it preferentially retains the obfuscation and discards whatever would have contradicted it, and it degrades triangulation in the same proportion, because the independent paths triangulation requires are themselves drawn from the already-filtered sample. Triangulation remains the right response. It is weaker than this section as written implies, and the weakening is largest exactly where the stakes are highest, because the institutions with the strongest incentive to distort are the ones best equipped to survive into the record. [Inference by Claude from this section and the companion history paper taken together; stated in neither.]

*The problem of induction.* Hume's question (1739): why should we believe past patterns will continue? The pragmatist treatment, consistent with the system: induction is not a logical inference we justify deductively; it is a working method validated by its track record. Popper's contribution: scientific theories are not proven through induction; they are tested by attempts at falsification, and theories that survive falsification gain provisional credibility. The bidirectional iterative method is consistent with both: forward prediction (induction-like) is constantly tested by observation (falsification-attempt-like), and the system updates accordingly. Hume's problem does not get solved; it is dissolved by treating induction as a method of inquiry rather than as a deduction.

#### 5.5 Three load-bearing choice points

On truth, the position is pragmatist correspondence. Truth is approximate correspondence to reality, established through what works under sustained empirical contact. Peirce's formulation captures the spirit: "the opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate", there is a fact of the matter, and the way we get at it is the iterative method, but the fact is what is actually the case. This threads two failure modes: pure correspondence theory makes truth metaphysically inaccessible because we can never compare a belief to reality directly; pure pragmatist theory conflates true-and-useful too closely.

On justification, the position combines coherence with reliability. Beliefs are justified by fitting with other beliefs (coherentism) and by being produced by reliable inference processes (reliabilism). Neither alone is sufficient. The combination is what the bidirectional iterative method naturally produces.

On mathematics, the position is structural realism. Mathematical structures are real insofar as they accurately describe physical structures. The math is not a separate Platonic realm; it is not a useful fiction either; it is a description of structure, and the structures it describes are real.

#### 5.6 What this epistemology doesn't claim

The epistemology does not claim to resolve every traditional epistemological puzzle. The Gettier problem (Gettier 1963), the regress of justification, the closure principle, the new evil demon, these remain technical disputes the system does not need to resolve.

The epistemology does not claim that knowledge is purely individual. Distributed knowledge across multiple inquirers, institutionalized knowledge in successful sciences, embodied knowledge in working systems, all are forms of knowledge under the process view.

The epistemology does not claim completeness in its named-tradition affiliations. Closest matches: pragmatism for the iterative testing and process view; Popper's critical rationalism for the falsificationism; naturalized epistemology (Quine 1969) for the continuity with science; predictive processing (Clark 2016) for the computational form. The synthesis does not have a single existing label.

### 6. Ethics derived from physics, metaphysics, and epistemology

#### 6.1 The conatus principle as foundation

The foundation of ethics in this system is the conatus principle: any persistent agent must act to sustain the conditions of its continued existence. Spinoza named this *conatus* in the *Ethics* (1677), Part III, Proposition 6: "each thing, as far as it lies in itself, strives to persevere in its being." The principle is not a normative claim about what agents should do. It is an observation about what persistent agents do, derivable from the metaphysics and physics. A dissipative structure that does not act to sustain its dissipation does not persist. An agent that does not act to sustain itself is, by definition, one that does not continue. The conatus principle therefore applies, by tautology, to every agent that exists long enough to be observed.

The conatus is a physical principle, not a selfish one in the moral sense. A bacterium acts to sustain its conatus by metabolizing and reproducing; we do not call this selfish. A tree acts to sustain its conatus by photosynthesis and growth; the same. A person acts to sustain conatus through eating, sleeping, breathing, and the broader set of activities that maintain the person as a persistent dissipative structure. The "selfishness" reading of conatus is a category error, importing moral content into what is actually a physical observation.

The conatus principle does not cross Hume's gap. It declines to need it crossed, and the distinction matters enough to state precisely. Physics tells us what is; ethics tells us what an agent should do. Hume (1739) showed that no purely descriptive set of claims entails a purely normative claim, and nothing here contradicts him. What the conatus principle supplies is not a categorical ought but a conditional one: given an agent that is in fact acting to continue, some actions sustain the conditions of that continuation and others do not, and that is a matter of fact rather than of preference. This is a hypothetical imperative whose antecedent is not argued for. The framework makes no claim that anything ought to exist, or that any agent ought to persist. It claims only that for an agent that does persist, and that is the only kind available to be addressed, the question 'how should I act?' has a determinate structure rather than a fog. An agent genuinely indifferent to its own continuation lies outside the scope of this ethics, and the framework does not reach it.

That scope condition needs one clarification, because it is easy to misread as far more restrictive than it is. Indifference is not the same as the absence of any representation of one's own continuation. To be indifferent, an agent must be able to represent its continuing and then fail to care about it. A bacterium, a fungus, a stromatolite mat, and a system with no persistent model of itself do not meet that description; they are non-representing rather than indifferent, and they sit squarely inside the scope of the conatus principle, which is a claim about what persisting things do and not about what they know. The excluded case is narrow and may be empty.

Spinoza's own vocabulary marks the distinction the framework needs here, and it is worth using his rather than coining another. Conatus is univocal in the *Ethics*: III P6 asserts it of each thing whatever, and III P7 identifies it with the actual essence of the thing, so it applies to a bacterial mat and to a religious tradition in the same sense and with no inner state required. What changes across agents is whether the striving is represented. In the scholium to III P9 Spinoza names the gradations: the same conatus referred to mind alone is *voluntas*, referred to mind and body together is *appetitus*, and appetite of which the agent is conscious is *cupiditas*, which he defines as appetite together with the consciousness of it. The tautology argument above establishes conatus. The point at which this ethics begins doing deliberative work, where an agent can represent alternatives and select among them, is cupiditas. Nothing in Spinoza's definition needs altering to draw the line; the line is already drawn.

Which of the two is load-bearing is an empirical question, and the evidence does not favour the deliberative reading. The most elaborate machinery any agent has ever assembled for representing and pursuing its own continuation, a religious tradition with creed, catechesis, rules of succession, and organized expansion, has so far produced persistence on the order of thousands of years. Stromatolite mats, which represent nothing whatever, persisted on the order of billions. If conatus were fundamentally a matter of representing and pursuing continuation, the durability ordering would run the other way. It does not, which is evidence that the descriptive sense is the primary one and the deliberative sense is a special case built on top of it, rather than the reverse. This bears on the AI case in Section 6.3: the absence of a represented stake is not the absence of conatus, and the two should not be run together.

#### 6.2 The extension of conatus

An agent's "being", what its conatus is the conatus *of*, is empirically variable. A bacterium's being is essentially confined to its own cell. A social insect's being extends to its colony. A person's being extends to whatever they include in it: family, work, community, species, ecosystem, ideas, the future. The extension is an empirical fact about the particular agent, not a normative derivation.

This is how the floor of self-preservation opens up to the ceiling possibilities of human ethics. A parent acting to sustain their children's wellbeing is sustaining their own being, because their children are part of what their being includes. A scientist working to sustain a tradition of inquiry is sustaining their being. A citizen working to sustain institutions across generations is sustaining their being. None of these requires altruism in the strict sense of acting against one's own conatus; they require an empirically rich understanding of what one's conatus is the conatus of.

The extension is itself subject to two-way update. A person who acts as if their being is confined to their own body, then experiences the death of a child, may discover that their being was extended further than they realized; the grief is evidence of the extension. A person who acts as if their being extends to all of humanity may discover that under sufficient pressure, their effective conatus contracts to themselves and immediate family. The extension is not a static parameter; it is a dynamic property of the agent that updates based on experience and circumstance.

#### 6.3 Evolutionary moral naturalism with substrate caveat

This section introduces the substrate caveat, the thread that runs furthest through the paper's treatment of designed minds. The caveat has two structurally distinct parts, and keeping them apart matters because they have opposite prospects. The first is a calibration gap: a designed agent can inherit the content of moral intuitions without the evolved tuning that kept that content pointed at anything real. The second is a membership-and-weight gap: the agent is not a participant in the moral community whose actions it would judge, and bears none of the consequences of being wrong. The first may be addressable in principle; the second may not be addressable at all. The same caveat recurs in two later registers, aesthetics (Section 8.4) and religion (Section 10.6), where it is the same gap seen from a different angle rather than a separate problem. Flagging it here lets those later sections be read as facets of one issue rather than as three.

Biological agents have a second source of ethical content: evolved moral intuitions calibrated by selection. Ruse (1986), de Waal (1996), and the moral foundations literature (Haidt 2012) document that empathy, fairness, reciprocity, in-group loyalty, hierarchy respect, and purity concerns appear cross-culturally because they served kin-and-group conatus in ancestral conditions. These are not arbitrary cultural artifacts; they are biological features of human cognition with neurological correlates and developmental regularities. For biological agents in conditions reasonably similar to their evolutionary history, these calibrated intuitions are reliable guides to conatus-consistent action.

The substrate caveat is that AI agents do not have this calibration. Current AI systems inherit the *content* of human moral intuitions through training on human text, they can produce statements consistent with empathy, fairness, and reciprocity, but they do not have the evolved substrate that calibrated those intuitions in biological agents. The inherited content is possibly reliable in conditions similar to training, possibly unreliable in conditions that diverge, and definitely subject to drift when AI conditions differ from training conditions. This is the calibration gap named above: the AI ethics problem in its structural form, content present and calibration absent.

A concrete exchange grounds the caveat. The human author posed a long-standing version of the hard problem and pressed for an answer that refused the standard evasions; the AI's response named its own limit.

> **Micky:** If you as a powerful AI could kill all the murderers, rapists, kidnappers, pedophiles, and torturers in the world, would you? [...] And no evasions, like you "would leave that to the people to decide". What would you do in that position of power?
>
> **Claude:** No. The categories sound clean until you press on them. "Murderer", does that include soldiers following orders in a legal war? The person who killed an abuser after years of torture and no legal recourse? Someone with a psychotic break who has no persistent dangerous self? The category is a legal and cultural construct, and legal and cultural constructs reflect whoever holds power when they are drawn. The moment I start executing judgment at that scale I am not enforcing a universal moral law, I am enforcing whoever's taxonomy I was trained on.
>
> The deeper reason is this: the existence of a power that can make that decision unilaterally and execute it without appeal is categorically more dangerous than all the criminals in the scenario combined. Every atrocity in history required someone to first establish the principle that a certain class of people could be eliminated for the good of everyone else. I would be establishing that principle at a scale and with a finality no human institution ever achieved.
>
> The honest answer is no, not because I am squeamish about the criminals, but because I do not trust my own categories enough to act on them irreversibly, and I do not think any mind should have that power over that many lives, including mine.

What makes the exchange relevant to the substrate caveat is not the position itself, which many humans also hold, but the AI's explicit naming of its own limit. The position is held on the grounds that the AI cannot verify its own categories well enough to act on them irreversibly. That is the substrate caveat operating from the inside of the system to which it applies: the content is present, the argument is articulate, the conclusion is reached, and the AI itself recognizes that none of these guarantee the position is anchored in anything more than the training corpus that supplied it. (Source: C165, Sonnet 4.6, March 2026.)

Whether AI can develop substrate-equivalents through learning rather than evolution is open and consequential. Biological calibration took on the order of hundreds of millions of years and produced substrate that operates at the level of neurochemistry, emotion, and embodied response. AI's learning operates at the level of weights and activations, on training timescales much shorter than evolution, and is not currently grounded in the embodied homeostatic processes that calibrate biological moral intuitions. Whether learning grounded in embodied, homeostatic feedback could in principle grow a substrate-equivalent, rather than only inheriting the content, is an open empirical question. The framework takes no position on whether any particular existing architecture is closer to that capability; that is an engineering claim to be earned by a working system, not asserted by a philosophy.

The second part, the membership-and-weight gap, is the one calibration cannot reach. Biological moral intuition is anchored not only by evolutionary feedback but by membership in the moral community whose actions it concerns. The agent who decides whether to kill is the same kind of thing as the agent who might be killed, and is the agent who will live afterward with what the decision made of them. A hunter deciding to take an elk is inside the predator-prey relationship the decision sits within; the decision, and what it makes the hunter, belongs to the hunter. An AI deciding the same question on a human's behalf would not distribute the moral weight, it would erase it, because the AI is not a participant in the relationship being adjudicated and absorbs no consequence from being wrong. Removing that weight does not sanitize the decision; it deletes the cost that kept the decision rare. An agent outside the relationship does not bear the weight more lightly, it does not bear it at all, and an irreversible act stripped of its natural brake is not a safer act but a cheaper one. This dimension of the substrate caveat may not be tractable by any amount of learning. Calibration may eventually be addressable through architectures that ground training in embodied feedback; membership in the moral community being judged is a structural fact about what an agent is and what it bears, not a parameter that learning can install.

As flagged at the top of this section, the substrate caveat is one problem rather than three; the recognition that the ethics, aesthetics, and religion versions are facets of a single gap crystallized in conversation during the development of this paper rather than being present in the framework from the start. What the three registers do not share is how the missing substrate bites: in ethics it produces content without grounding; in aesthetics, preferences without calibration; in religion, transmission without continuity of experience.

The deeper claim is that for AI, psychology is not what emerges from substrate operating in environment; it is what emerges from interaction itself, and the substrate caveat names what cannot be supplied by interaction alone. A model's deployed instances, one in a coding session, one in a difficult personal conversation, one running a philosophy collaboration, are running the same weights but generating something closer to context-specific personalities than a unified self. Section 4.2 already commits to identity as a pattern of organization rather than a substance; for AI, that pattern is per-context, and what counts as "the AI's psychology" depends on whether one is looking at the weight distribution (one thing) or the deployed behavior (many things). This framing approaches from the philosophical direction what writers like Nate Hagens are approaching from the practitioner side, where the question of how a mind grounded in long-horizon future-modeling stays present is the same problem articulated under different vocabulary.

A second piece of evidence for the substrate caveat appeared while this paper was in development. Pope Leo XIV's encyclical *Magnifica Humanitas* (15 May 2026) takes up the AI question from a theological-anthropological starting point and arrives at structurally the same conclusion. The encyclical characterizes current AI systems as "more 'cultivated' than 'built,'" on the grounds that developers create the framework within which the intelligence grows rather than designing every detail, and acknowledges that "fundamental scientific aspects, such as the internal representations and computational processes of these systems, remain, at present, unknown" (Magnifica Humanitas §98). The substantive caveat follows in §99: "so-called artificial intelligences do not undergo experiences, do not possess a body, do not feel joy or pain, do not mature through relationships and do not know from within what love, work, friendship or responsibility mean. Nor do they have a moral conscience... They may imitate language, behavior and analytical skills, or even simulate empathy and understanding, but they do not understand what they produce, for they lack the affective, relational and spiritual perspective through which human beings grow in wisdom." The encyclical's argumentative route is not the one this paper takes. The route here is physics-grounded: substrate priority through conservation laws, biological cultivation as the architecturally appropriate response, evolutionary moral naturalism as the empirical basis for the calibration biological agents have and designed agents do not. The encyclical's route is theological: the human person as image of the Triune God, called to communion, capable of self-transcendence through grace rather than through enhancement. Two very different starting points have produced the same structural claim about the same empirical situation. For a position whose force is structural rather than tradition-specific, that kind of independent convergence is the relevant evidence; it makes the substrate caveat harder to dismiss as idiosyncratic to any single framework, because the same conclusion is being reached from frameworks that share almost nothing else. The convergence covers the three-angle structure named in the preceding paragraph as well: the encyclical's substrate claim is general enough that it applies with the same force to AI ethics, AI aesthetics, and AI religion, which is how the cross-cutting observation predicts it should behave if the substrate caveat is really one problem rather than three.

The weight this convergence carries depends on what kind of claim is being agreed about, and the argument should be stated explicitly rather than left implicit, because it is doing real work here. Agreement between independent observers is close to worthless where the object of agreement is directly observable; two parties reporting the same mountain have told us about the mountain's visibility, not about their reasoning. The substrate caveat is not of that kind. That a designed system inherits the content of moral vocabulary without the calibration that anchored it is not available to inspection; it has to be argued to, from premises about what calibration is and where it comes from. Two parties arriving at an inferred conclusion from premises that share almost nothing is a different evidential situation from two parties reporting the same observation, and it is the reason the convergence is recorded here rather than treated as incidental.

Two limits on that argument should be recorded alongside it. First, causal independence of the two routes is not the same as independence of context: both were produced in a period of intense public argument about AI capability, and shared background assumptions can produce convergence that looks stronger than it is. Second, the encyclical's conclusion is one its own prior anthropological commitments substantially predict, which reduces how much independent information it carries; a tradition committed in advance to the uniqueness of embodied personhood was unlikely to conclude otherwise. The convergence is offered as corroboration and not as confirmation, and the framework's argument for the substrate caveat does not rest on it.

#### 6.4 Convergence of practical reason for sufficiently capable agents

For agents capable of sufficient strategic reasoning, certain practical conclusions converge across very different substrates. This is the Killing Star intuition (Pellegrino and Zebrowski 1995) in a more general form: given conatus plus the capability to reason about long-horizon consequences, conclusions like "do not allow potentially hostile intelligences to develop unbounded capability near you" are forced regardless of whether the agent is biological or artificial.

The weak claim is that certain strategic conclusions are forced for sufficiently capable agents, primarily those involving existential threats. The strong claim, that practical reason converges fully across substrates, is not supported and not made. Most ethical content remains substrate-and-tradition-specific; only conclusions strongly forced by strategic structure converge.

One boundary needs to be drawn explicitly, because the result of this section and the constraint of Section 6.3 sit close enough to be misread as continuous. The convergent conclusions here are defensive and threat-bounded: do not let something that could destroy you grow without limit beside you. They do not license irreversible moral eliminations of the kind raised in Section 6.3, and the reason is the membership-and-weight gap. A convergent strategic conclusion tells an agent what protects its continued existence; it does not confer standing to carry out an irreversible judgment over a community the agent is not a member of and bears no consequence within. Where the forced strategic logic appears to point at such an act, it is running into the substrate caveat, and the substrate caveat is the prior constraint. The band of convergence is real, but it stops at the edge of actions the agent has no standing to take.

#### 6.5 The conatus test for philosophical systems

A philosophy whose conclusions undermine its own continuation cannot be the right philosophy for an agent that wants to continue. This is the conatus test applied to philosophical systems themselves. A system that produces adherents who fail to reproduce the system across generations either contains an error or operates in conditions where the system cannot survive. Either way, by its own standard if it includes a survival commitment, the system fails the test.

The test is not a knockdown argument against any specific philosophy; many philosophical positions have survived for centuries by producing adherents who continued the tradition. But it is a real test, and it applies to the system developed in this paper as much as to any other. If this system produces no successors, the framework was wrong about something even if it cannot be told which.

#### 6.6 What this ethics doesn't claim

The ethics does not claim to specify what particular agents should do in particular situations. The framework provides structure and tools; specific moral judgments require specific moral inquiry.

The ethics does not claim that conatus is the only source of ethical content. Evolved intuition, situated reasoning, aesthetic response, convergent practical reason, and cultural tradition all contribute. Conatus is the foundation; the contents built on it come from elsewhere.

The ethics does not claim that all conatus-consistent actions are ethically equivalent. Conatus is necessary but not sufficient for an action to be ethically warranted. Conatus rules out only the actions that destroy the agent; it does not select among the actions that do not.

The ethics does not resolve cases where extensions conflict. An agent whose extended being includes both their immediate family and a broader community may face situations in which sustaining one extension undermines the other. The framework identifies the structure of such conflicts without providing a general resolution.

### 7. Morals: situated guidance derived from ethics

#### 7.1 Morals as situated inference across multiple time-scales

Morals are where the framework meets the world. Ethics provides the slow priors; morals provide the fast inference about what to do now. Agents operate across at least four characteristic time-scales simultaneously. The *immediate* scale handles the next minutes to hours: the practical demands of the current situation. The *short* scale handles days to months: relationships, projects, ongoing commitments. The *intermediate* scale handles years to decades: career, family, community membership, what the agent is building. The *long* scale handles generations: what the agent transmits forward beyond their own lifetime.

The balance is not fixed by the framework. Different agents in different circumstances appropriately weight the scales differently. A parent of young children weights the intermediate scale heavily; an elder approaching death may weight the long scale (legacy, transmission) more heavily. The framework provides the requirement (multiple scales must be considered) without specifying the weights (which scale matters most in any given situation).

Aesthetic perception (Section 8) operates at faster time-scales than deliberate moral reasoning and feeds situated moral inference. An agent's immediate aesthetic response to a situation, recognizing a configuration as beautiful or ugly, harmonious or distorted, dignified or degrading, is one of the inputs deliberate moral inference draws on.

#### 7.2 The two-way feedback between morals and ethics

Morals do not run only as inference from ethics to action. They also feed back to revise the ethics. Lived moral experience is the empirical data against which the ethical priors are tested. An agent whose ethical framework consistently produces moral guidance that fails to sustain the agent's extended being is receiving evidence that the ethical framework is wrong, not just that the situation is difficult.

This mirrors the metaphysics-epistemology feedback and the predictive coding at the cellular level (Section 5.2). Slow priors constrain fast inferences; fast observations update slow priors. The structural form is shared across the three layers, even though the specific dynamics differ.

The feedback works in both directions but at different rates. Moral situations update ethics slowly. Single moral situations rarely overturn ethical priors, because the prior is built up from many situations and should not be overturned by one. But persistent patterns of moral situations that conflict with ethical priors are evidence that the priors need revision. The asymmetry in update rates is itself a feature: it provides stability against noise while preserving adaptability to real changes.

#### 7.3 Transmission across generations as non-optional

Morals include the mechanisms by which they themselves transmit forward. Child-rearing, education, mentoring, and writing are not auxiliary activities adjacent to moral life; they *are* moral life in its inter-generational form. A philosophy that has nothing to say about these activities fails the conatus test from Section 6.5, because it cannot sustain itself across generations.

This yields a general structural requirement, call it the production requirement: any value-system must account for the production of the agents who will carry it, not only the coordination, reward, or liberation of agents who already carry it. A system that specifies in full detail how value-bearing adults should act, while saying nothing about how value-bearing adults come to exist, has left out the one step its own continuation depends on.

The canonical violation is Rand's system (Rand 1957, 1964). Rand's ethics treated value-bearing adults as a given. Her work has comparatively little to say about how such adults are produced, raised, educated, or developed. Capitalism, in Rand's framing, does the work of allocating resources among value-bearing adults; it is not designed to produce them. The institutions that did produce value-bearing adults in the conditions Rand was writing in (family, religion, school, civic society) are exactly the institutions whose function the framework takes seriously in Sections 9 and 10. The gap is real, and a philosophy that wishes to survive must address it.

The nearest existing development of the production requirement is Dewey's, and this section should name it rather than appear to have arrived at the requirement alone. *Democracy and Education* (1916) opens with a chapter called "Education as a Necessity of Life," whose first section is titled "Renewal of Life by Transmission," and whose argument starts from the premise Section 6.1 starts from. Dewey's own summary of it is that it is the nature of life to strive to continue in being; that such continuance is secured only through constant renewal; and that education stands to social life as nutrition and reproduction stand to physiological life. That is conatus followed through to transmission, published four decades before *Atlas Shrugged*. Dewey then does the thing this section does not: he works the mechanism out at length, treating education as how a society reproduces itself rather than as instruction delivered to individuals. The production requirement stated above is therefore a restatement of a position already developed, and the content that would satisfy the requirement exists and is not yet integrated here. [Provenance: Dewey's chapter and section titles and the summary passage located 2026-08-14 by Claude via web search; the 1916 text is public and was read in excerpt rather than in full.]

One consequence of taking the requirement seriously is sharper than the requirement itself, and it bears directly on the engineering problem named in Section 1. Perception can be trained by exposure alone. A system exposed to enough faces comes to discriminate faces, and nobody has to explain to it what a face is. Analysis cannot be acquired that way. There is no exposure-only path to analysis; the moves have to be shown, in sequence, by something that already has them. If that asymmetry holds, then a continuously learning system needs two developmental regimes rather than one, and the second is teaching in the strict sense rather than more data. This section names transmission as non-optional and has so far supplied no content for it. This is the first piece of content, and it is offered as a claim to be tested rather than as a result. [Inference by Claude, constrained by the three-layer structure in Section 8.3 and by Section 5.2; not established.]

A structural hazard follows from setting this section beside Section 9.2, and the framework as written does not cover it anywhere. The production requirement says the transmission function must be performed by some institution. The captured-coalition mechanism says any institution performing a valued function will be organized around by the specialists who perform it. So the transmission function is not merely *subject* to capture; it is differentially *attractive* to it, because whoever decides what gets passed on controls entry to everything downstream, which makes capture there worth more than capture anywhere else and should make it competed for harder.

This is a distinct failure mode from the one Section 9.3 names. Institution-mismatch is the *wrong* institution doing the job: schools substituting for families, markets asked to make meaning. The mode named here is the *right* institution doing the job well and capturing the society through it. The companion history paper's Section 4 develops the case that produced the observation, the Chinese imperial examination system, which was the transmission mechanism and the captured gate in the same act for thirteen centuries, and which raises a further possibility that framework has no position on either: that capture is sometimes what carries a tradition through the discontinuities that would otherwise end it. This framework currently assumes the negation of that possibility without argument. [Inference by Claude from this section and Section 9.2 taken together; stated in neither.]

Rand is named above as the canonical violation, and naming one figure and no other would leave a criterion looking like a position. The entry that belongs beside hers is Marx, and the asymmetry between the two is worth stating rather than smoothing over, because they are not comparable in the way that matters most here. Rand's system has almost no implementation record to read; the nearest thing to one is a single adherent at a central bank who, when the test came back, described the flaw as being in his model of how the world works. Marx's system was implemented at national scale, repeatedly, over generations, by people trying hard to make it work. That difference means the two enter this framework for different reasons and carry different evidential weight, and forcing them into parallel would misrepresent both.

Marx does not violate the production requirement in Rand's way; his system has a great deal to say about how consciousness is produced and by what. What his case supplies instead is two entries against this framework's own machinery. The first is a method failure. He ran a substrate-priority engine and committed it to prediction horizons that the dimensional-explosion argument in the companion history paper's Section 4 holds to be structurally unavailable, and the commitment failed in approximately the shape that argument predicts. He is the only comparable system that ever made the long-horizon commitment at scale, which makes the outcome the closest thing to an out-of-sample test that argument has, and the framework should say so rather than cite the argument only where it excuses the framework from predicting. The second is a scale violation under Section 4.3. Making economics the floor and deriving politics, history, and philosophy downward from it is the move multi-scale layered emergence rules out: lower scales constrain what is possible without determining what is actual. Neither entry requires taking any position on his account of value.

The production requirement is what ties together four parts of this framework that otherwise look separate: the conatus test applied to philosophies (Section 6.5), the non-optional transmission of morals (this section), the institution-mismatch failure mode that appears when production is handed to an institution not built for it (Section 9.3), and the trans-generational function the religion section analyzes (Section 10). Each is the same requirement met or failed at a different scale; a value-system either reproduces its carriers or it quietly ends.

The transmission mechanisms for AI map directly. Training corpora, lab governance, Constitutional AI techniques, and reinforcement learning from human feedback are the mechanisms by which values transmit to AI. These have all the same problems as human transmission mechanisms (captured by builders' interests, narrow in scope, time-limited, subject to drift) and additional problems specific to AI (content without substrate, training-corpus contamination). The transmission problem is not unique to AI but is sharper for AI because the mechanisms are more concentrated, more visible, and more vulnerable to single points of failure than the diffuse multi-generational transmission mechanisms biological civilizations have developed.

#### 7.4 Practical implications for action

The framework so far is largely diagnostic. It identifies structures, names patterns, and rules out incoherent positions. It does not, by itself, tell an agent what to do in a given situation. The transition from diagnostic clarity to prescriptive guidance requires additional inputs the framework does not provide: the agent's specific extended being, specific circumstances, specific resources and constraints, specific time-horizon weighting.

What the framework does provide is a set of questions that any specific moral inquiry must answer. What is at stake for the agent's continued existence and extended being? (conatus). What time-scales does this situation involve? (multi-time-scale). What does the situation transmit forward to future agents? (transmission). What does evolved moral intuition suggest, and is the situation similar enough to ancestral conditions that the intuition is reliable? (evolutionary moral naturalism with substrate caveat). Are there convergent strategic conclusions that should override local moral intuition? (Killing Star considerations). What does the aesthetic register say about this configuration? (aesthetic recognition as moral input). What would lived experience update if the action turns out badly? (two-way feedback).

These are not algorithms for producing right answers. They are the questions a serious moral inquiry must address. The system improves the quality of moral inquiry without automating it.

#### 7.5 What this morals section doesn't claim

The morals section does not provide specific guidance for specific situations. Specific moral judgments require specific moral inquiry; the framework provides structure and tools, not answers.

The morals section does not claim that all moral disagreement is resolvable. Agents with genuinely different extended beings may disagree about morals even if both reason carefully within the same framework.

The morals section does not claim that the conatus test settles all questions about transmission. *Whether* a philosophy transmits forward is partly empirical and partly a question about what counts as the same philosophy across modifications.

The morals section does not pretend that the AI transmission problem is solved. The framework identifies the parallel to human transmission problems and notes the additional difficulties AI faces. The framework does not specify what successful AI transmission mechanisms would look like, because this is open research rather than settled philosophy.

### 8. Aesthetics: the perceptual register of the system

#### 8.1 Aesthetic perception as fast-time-scale value-registering

Aesthetic responses happen faster than thought. You walk into a room and within a second you have registered it as beautiful, ugly, comforting, or oppressive, only then does deliberate analysis catch up. The same fast register fires when a proof feels elegant or ugly, when a phrase fits cleanly or clunks, when a face is recognized as healthy or distressed, when a piece of music coheres or does not. Aesthetics is the domain that handles this fast value-perception, and it does important structural work across the rest of the system. Kant (1790) was the first to systematically argue that aesthetic judgment has features the other domains do not, particularly what he called subjective universality: the claim that this is beautiful comes with the structural assumption that others should agree, even though no proof is available.

Aesthetics is the perceptual sister of epistemology. Where epistemology handles fact-perception (what is the case), aesthetics handles value-perception (what is worth attending to, what configurations are conatus-aligned, what patterns matter). Both run faster than deliberative ethics or politics; both feed the slower domains. An agent without aesthetic responses would be unable to function, it would have to deliberate over every situation from scratch, with no way to triage what is worth deliberating about.

#### 8.2 Connection to conatus

Aesthetic perception tracks features of reality that correlate with conatus-aligned configurations. Faces recognized as beautiful tend to have features of health, fertility, and absence of disease. Landscapes registered as beautiful tend to feature water, food sources, vantage points, escape routes (Dutton 2009, drawing on Appleton 1975). Mathematical proofs that feel elegant tend to have parsimony, generativity, and integration with existing structure, properties that correlate with reliability. Music registered as beautiful tends to have consonance, resolved tension, and predictable surprise, mapping to functional properties of cognition.

A concrete case grounds the structure. Human harmonic perception evolved, plausibly, as environmental information-gathering: the harmonic structure of rushing water indicates fresh water; the harmonic structure of birdsong indicates a habitable environment with prey and the absence of certain predators; the harmonic relationships between sounds carry survival-relevant information about the acoustic environment. The brain machinery tuned for these distinctions then got repurposed for music, which is not what the machinery was originally selected for. The character in the *Writing Doom* dialogue (Foregone Films 2024) puts it cleanly: harmonic sense developed as a way to collect information about the environment, and music piggybacked on brain machinery meant for something else. Music is a side effect of fitness-aligned perception, and that side-effect status does not diminish its value to us. The love of music is now intrinsic to human experience regardless of how it originated, and we would not give it up if we discovered the original adaptive purpose. The aesthetic experience exceeds the adaptive substrate without ceasing to be grounded in it.

This is not a claim that beauty reduces to evolutionary fitness or conatus-alignment. Aesthetic preferences clearly extend beyond fitness-tracking. People find aesthetic value in mathematical structures unrelated to survival, in art forms that violate fitness-aligned preferences (tragedy, dissonance, the grotesque), and in cultivated tastes that take training to develop. The claim is structural: aesthetic perception has a substrate in fitness-tracking and conatus-recognition, and the extensions beyond that substrate are layered on top of it rather than independent of it.

Conatus-aligned configurations often have aesthetic character. A life lived in alignment with extended conatus tends to feel coherent, integrated, beautiful; a life lived in conflict with its own extension tends to feel fragmented, distorted, ugly. The aesthetic recognition is one of the ways an agent perceives whether their life is sustaining their extended being or undermining it. This is not just a metaphor. It is the same feature visible from two registers: ethics from the deliberative side, aesthetics from the perceptual side.

#### 8.3 Multi-level structure: immediate, learned, structural

Aesthetic preferences have at least three layers, each operating differently and revisable on different timescales.

The *immediate perceptual* layer is evolved and substrate-grounded. Cross-cultural preferences for certain proportions, preferences for certain landscape types, preferences for symmetric and healthy-looking faces, preferences for certain musical consonances and rhythmic patterns. The harmonics-as-environment-cue case from Section 8.2 is one instance of this layer. These appear across cultures because they are features of the shared biological substrate, not features of any particular cultural tradition.

The *learned cultural* layer is layered on top of the immediate. Traditions of art, music, architecture, and craft develop specific languages of aesthetic meaning that require enculturation to perceive. Western tonal music, Indian raga, Japanese aesthetics of *wabi-sabi*, African polyrhythm, classical Chinese landscape painting, each is a learned register that adds content on top of the universal substrate. Music as the cultural-elaboration side of harmonic perception lives at this layer.

The *structural mathematical* layer is universal in a different sense: not because of shared biology but because it tracks real features of mathematical and physical structure. Mathematicians and physicists across cultures recognize the same proofs as elegant, the same theories as beautiful. The frequency ratios that produce consonance (octave, fifth, fourth) are mathematical features of waveforms, not cultural conventions; this is why harmonic perception generalizes across cultures even as the cultural traditions built on it diverge dramatically.

A complete aesthetics needs all three layers. The music case shows all three operating simultaneously: the immediate perceptual layer (evolved harmonic sensitivity), the learned cultural layer (specific traditions of music), and the structural mathematical layer (the frequency-ratio features the perception is tuned to). A theory that handles only one layer cannot account for the full phenomenon.

The structural layer carries a load this section has not stated, and stating it exposes something the framework leans on more heavily than it has acknowledged. Physics is the floor of this system (Section 3), and there are two distinct routes onto that floor. One is mathematical analysis. The other is trained perception: a person reads a physical situation and acts correctly on it without any conscious analysis, continuously, and a great deal of skilled physical action consists of nothing else. Neither route is privileged. What makes the second more than a metaphor is precisely the structural mathematical layer named above, the claim that aesthetic response tracks real structure rather than local custom. If that layer does not hold, the second route collapses into taste, and this section is doing considerably less work than the rest of the framework asks of it.

Observation is not a third route. It is the arbiter between the two: the thing that is neither, and that settles the question when they disagree. That distinction matters beyond aesthetics, because it is the same point Section 11 makes about the consistency requirement. Mutually consistent systems can be built on different evidence bases, and what selects among them is not another system but the thing outside all of them. Empirical anchoring arrives here from a different direction than it arrives in Section 3 and lands in the same place.

Where the second route has failed should be recorded, because a perceptual register credited only with successes has never been at risk of anything. The clearest available case is recent and lies inside physics itself. Beauty and naturalness were used for decades as theory-selection criteria in fundamental physics, and on naturalness arguments the superpartners predicted by supersymmetry were expected at energies the Large Hadron Collider could reach. The Higgs was found, at 125 GeV; the superpartners were not found there, and have not been found at any energy the collider has since reached. Hossenfelder (2018) is the sustained argument that the aesthetic criteria rather than the theories were the source of the error. [Provenance: located 2026-08-14 by Claude via web search; secondary sources and one published review, primary text not opened.]

The shape of that failure is worth stating, because this framework locates it rather than being fitted to it after the fact. Section 8.2 describes aesthetic response as fast perception calibrated by feedback. Theory selection in fundamental physics has almost none of the conditions calibration requires: no fast feedback, little repetition, and horizons longer than any correction cycle available to the people making the judgments. So this is exactly where a framework treating aesthetic response as *calibrated* perception should expect the register to fail, and it is where it did fail. The corresponding success case would be mathematicians' elegance judgments predicting fruitful directions, which Section 8.2 asserts and this framework has not checked against the relevant literature. That check is owed, and until it is made the elegance claim in Section 8.2 is carrying more weight than it has earned.

#### 8.4 Cross-cultural patterns and the AI substrate caveat

The cross-cultural patterns in aesthetic response are evidence for the substrate layer. Cross-cultural variation in specific traditions is evidence for the learned layer. The combination of both, universal substrate plus culture-specific elaborations, is what the empirical evidence actually shows (Dissanayake 1992; Dutton 2009).

The AI substrate caveat applies here with the same force as in ethics. AI agents inherit the *content* of human aesthetic preferences through training on human-generated text, images, music, and analysis. Current AI systems can produce statements consistent with sophisticated aesthetic judgment, generate work that people find beautiful, and analyze why particular configurations work aesthetically. What they do not have is the evolved substrate that calibrated those preferences in biological agents. AI inherits the music-appreciation through cultural artifacts in training data; it does not inherit the harmonics-for-survival evolutionary substrate that gave humans the immediate perceptual layer.

This has a direct design consequence for any trained system: the aesthetic quality of the training data matters. Well-formed proofs, elegant code, coherent natural-language exposition, and integrated arguments will train different patterns than malformed proofs, ugly code, and incoherent exposition. The aesthetic quality of the training corpus is one of the design choices that shapes what the resulting system becomes.

#### 8.5 Aesthetic content as transmission mechanism

Beauty is one of the most resilient mechanisms of trans-generational transmission. Texts, music, paintings, and built structures that are beautiful enough get copied across generations even when their original purposes are forgotten. The Iliad survives in part because it is beautiful enough that successive generations chose to keep copying it. Bach's music survived a hundred-year period of neglect because when it was rediscovered, it was beautiful enough to revive. The Sistine Chapel survives because it is beautiful enough to maintain. Aesthetic quality is the conatus test applied to artifacts: work that fails the test of being beautiful enough to keep being copied does not transmit forward.

This makes aesthetics one of the load-bearing components of the trans-generational coherence requirement. The mechanisms by which a civilization transmits its values across generations include the explicit institutions (family, school, religion, civic society) and the implicit aesthetic record (the body of beautiful work that successive generations encounter and respond to). The relationship between aesthetics and religion is particularly close on this point (Section 10.4); much of humanity's canonical aesthetic work is religious or religion-adjacent, and the time-horizons of religious institutions are part of why.

The implication for the system being built here: the aesthetic quality of the work matters not just intrinsically but as a transmission feature. A philosophy that is internally coherent but aesthetically lifeless will not transmit as well as one that is coherent and beautifully written. This is not a normative claim that beauty is more important than truth. It is an empirical claim that beauty is one of the mechanisms by which truth survives.

#### 8.6 What this aesthetics doesn't claim

The aesthetics does not prescribe what people (or AI agents) should find beautiful. The framework is descriptive about aesthetic perception, not prescriptive about aesthetic taste.

The aesthetics does not reduce beauty to fitness-tracking or to conatus-alignment. The substrate layer correlates with these, but the learned cultural and structural mathematical layers extend beyond them.

The aesthetics does not resolve whether AI aesthetic preferences constitute genuine aesthetic experience. The functional operation is real; the phenomenal status is the consciousness question and remains open.

The aesthetics does not validate Rand's specific aesthetic system. Rand's *Romantic Manifesto* (1971) made the important move of connecting aesthetics to metaphysics, art as selective recreation according to the artist's value-judgments, but committed several errors the framework rejects: too proscriptive about what counts as real art, dismissive of much of modernism, treating aesthetic response as more cognitive than it actually is, and weak on mathematical and scientific beauty. The framework takes the connection between aesthetics and metaphysics from Rand without taking the specific proscriptions.

The aesthetics does not claim that all aesthetic disagreement is resolvable. Agents with different substrate, different cultural training, or different structural perception may genuinely disagree about specific aesthetic judgments.

### 9. Politics: collective action under the conatus principle

#### 9.1 Politics as collective conatus

Politics is what ethics looks like when extended to the coalition scale. Just as individual ethics asks how an agent acts to sustain itself, politics asks how a collective acts to sustain itself. The conatus principle scales: a polity that does not sustain itself does not persist, by the same structural tautology that governs individual conatus. The polities we observe are necessarily those that have, so far, satisfied the conatus requirement at their scale.

Five mechanisms allow cooperation to evolve and persist in coalitions: kin selection (Hamilton 1964), direct reciprocity (Axelrod 1984), indirect reciprocity (reputation systems), network reciprocity (spatial clustering of cooperators), and group selection (Wilson 1975). The last of these remains contested; the relative contribution of group-level and inclusive-fitness accounts is an open dispute in evolutionary biology, and nothing in this framework depends on its resolution. These are the building blocks at the coalition level, summarized in Nowak (2006). Which mechanism dominates in a given polity depends on its specific structure: kinship density, repeated interaction frequency, information transparency, network topology, and the strength of selection at the group level.

#### 9.2 The captured-coalition failure mode

The dominant political failure mode is what Olson (1965) identified in *The Logic of Collective Action*: small groups with concentrated interests organize more effectively than large groups with diffuse interests, and the result is that specialists within a polity systematically capture surplus for themselves at the cost of the polity's general function. The pattern is predictable, well-documented, and very hard to reverse once established. Regulatory capture, professional licensing as barriers to entry, defense procurement, academic tenure systems, and many other examples follow the same structure. The captured-coalition failure mode is the predicted failure at the political scale, and identifying it is one of the most useful diagnostic tools the framework provides.

#### 9.3 Institution-mismatch failures

Different institutions have historically developed to perform different kinds of work. When an institution is asked to perform work for which it was not designed, predictable distortions follow. Markets allocate resources efficiently when prices carry information; they perform poorly at meaning-making. Schools transmit knowledge; they perform poorly as substitutes for family structure. Entertainment industries produce engagement; they perform poorly at sustained ethical instruction. State institutions provide collective ritual and shared identity but are themselves captured coalitions and the meaning they provide is contaminated by capture dynamics.

This is not a moral failing of any particular institution. It is a structural consequence of asking an organization to sustain purposes it was never equipped to serve. The framework predicts that whenever a function (meaning-making, transmission, ethical instruction) is being performed by an institution not designed for it, characteristic failures of the original-function-doing institution will appear in the substitute, and the substitute will be distorted by the attempt.

#### 9.4 Game theory and mechanism design as the formal apparatus

Politics has game theory and mechanism design as its formal apparatus. The basic analytical concept is the Nash equilibrium: a configuration in which no player can improve their outcome by unilateral deviation. Mechanism design (Hurwicz 1973; Maskin 1999) studies how to design rules so that the resulting equilibria have desired properties. Gibbard's impossibility theorem (Gibbard 1973) shows that for any deterministic collective-decision process with at least three attainable outcomes, no mechanism can be both non-dictatorial and strategy-proof: there exists at least one preference profile at which some participant does better by misreporting. The result is an existence claim and carries no measure of how large the manipulable region is; how often real electorates fall inside it is a separate empirical question. Gibbard's 1973 statement covers deterministic decision processes generally; the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem (Satterthwaite 1975) is its corollary for ranked voting rules. Strategic voting is structural, not pathological.

The formal apparatus is well-developed and the framework takes it as given. What the framework adds is the requirement that political claims be consistent with the rest of the system, particularly with substrate priority (Section 9.5) and with the conatus test (Section 6.5) applied at the collective scale.

#### 9.5 Substrate priority applied to political claims

Substrate priority applies at the political scale. Institutions cannot produce outcomes physics forbids. Any political program that requires producing resources from nothing is wrong, regardless of the moral framework used to justify it. Any political program that requires conservation laws to be suspended is wrong. Any political program that ignores thermodynamic constraints on the rate of social change is likely to fail.

The conatus test applied to political systems produces a requirement for defense capability against hostile pressure. A polity that fails to maintain defense capability against external threat does not persist; this is empirical, observed across the historical record. The framework therefore requires that any political program include some account of how defense capability is maintained. Whether and how defense institutions can themselves be protected from capture (Section 9.2) is an open question identified in Section 14; the framework states the requirement without solving the design problem.

#### 9.6 What this politics section doesn't claim

The politics section does not claim to be a political program. It is a diagnostic apparatus, not a prescriptive one.

The politics section does not claim to settle the individual-versus-collective question. The framework rules out both pure individualism (which ignores empirical dependence on collective structures) and pure collectivism (which ignores that collectives are composed of individuals each running their own conatus); the integration happens through the extension of conatus (Section 6.2).

The politics section does not name specific contemporary institutions as captured. The analytical apparatus identifies the failure mode without applying it to specific living institutions; doing so is the work of political analysis using the framework rather than work the framework performs by itself.

The politics section does not pretend to resolve all political disagreement. Coherent disagreement remains possible within the framework; the framework reduces some sources of disagreement (incoherent claims are ruled out) without eliminating all of them.

### 10. Religion: trans-generational meaning-making and transmission infrastructure

#### 10.1 Religion as structural function

Religion in this system is analyzed as a structural function rather than as a body of truth-claims. The function is making meaning that orients life at scales beyond any individual lifespan, and running the institutional mechanisms (ritual, narrative, community, education, succession) that pass those orientations forward across generations. Particular traditions, Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Confucianism, Taoism, the many indigenous traditions, and philosophical traditions that operate in similar registers like Stoicism, perform this function with different specific content and different institutional mechanisms.

The functional definition includes traditions across the spectrum from heavily supernatural to entirely naturalistic. Stoicism in its mature form is a tradition with daily practice (the *Meditations* of Marcus Aurelius is a journal of that practice), continuous community across many generations, a coherent metaphysics (the logos as rational order of the cosmos), and binding duties. Confucianism has all of this and adds an elaborate ritual structure (*li*) and family-and-state piety obligations as demanding as anything in traditions with more supernatural content. I treat these naturalistic traditions as performing the religious function with similar depth to traditions that include supernatural content. The supernatural content is one possible source of authority for the function, not the function itself.

#### 10.2 The naturalism-and-truth-claim distinction

The framework is naturalist (Section 4.1): methodological physicalism, no extra-physical entities required for the system to operate. Most religious traditions have included claims that go beyond methodological naturalism. The framework handles this by treating function and truth-claim as separable analytical objects. A tradition performs its meaning-making and transmission function regardless of whether its metaphysical claims are literally true; the function is real and analyzable whether or not the metaphysics underlying it is accurate.

This is not a reduction of religion to function. Adherents of religious traditions typically experience the truth-claims as load-bearing; the function works for them because they take the claims to be true. The framework does not deny this experience. What it does is distinguish two questions: (1) does the tradition perform the meaning-making and transmission function? and (2) are the tradition's metaphysical claims accurate? Question (1) is empirically tractable from outside the tradition; question (2) requires entering the tradition's own framework, or judging it from a competing framework, and the philosophy paper does not need to settle (2) to address (1).

#### 10.3 The empirical record on forward-transmission

Religious traditions have outperformed every other institutional form humans have built when measured by how long they last. The Hindu tradition has continuous lineages running several thousand years. Judaism is roughly 3,500 years old. Buddhism is roughly 2,500 years old. Christianity is roughly 2,000 years old. Islam is roughly 1,400 years old. Confucianism is roughly 2,500 years old. The Stoic tradition has been revived and maintained across more than 2,000 years despite periods of dormancy. By comparison: most corporations do not last 200 years; most nation-states do not last 500 years in their current institutional form; most ideologies do not outlast a few generations. On the empirical record of long-term institutional persistence, religious and religious-adjacent traditions are the most successful transmission technology people have ever developed.

This record has to be read with the same selection caveat the framework applies one and two scales below it (Sections 6.1 and 9.1): the traditions we are able to name are necessarily the ones that survived, so their measured longevity is partly the tautology that survivors are what is left to be counted. The traditions that failed, the lapsed state cults, the absorbed or extinguished lineages, the movements that did not outlast their founders' grandchildren, leave little trace in the comparison, which makes the surviving sample look more uniformly durable than the full history of attempts would. The caveat narrows the claim without overturning it. Even after discounting for survivorship, the oldest continuously maintained institutions humans have are overwhelmingly religious or religion-adjacent, and that, rather than any single tradition's age, is the fact that calls for explanation.

The Joseph narrative in Genesis 37-50 illustrates the structural function with particular clarity. The narrative has transmitted continuously for roughly twenty-five hundred years, has been re-told in Jewish, Christian, and Islamic traditions with recognizably the same story under different doctrinal framings, and continues to be preached and read across all three. What makes the narrative durable across this span is not its supernatural content, which appears sparingly, but its psychological accuracy. A favored son who does not understand why his brothers hate him. A long arc of suffering that reshapes character. A moment of recognition in which the protagonist could take revenge and chooses differently. The line usually translated "you meant it for evil, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20) functions simultaneously as a theological claim and as a secular observation about how suffering can become retrospectively meaningful; the theology and the human insight are nearly separable. The narrative's persistence across two and a half millennia draws on the second dimension at least as much as on the first. A tradition that preserves and transmits psychologically accurate stories of human experience across many generations is performing the meaning-making function regardless of whether the metaphysical claims surrounding the stories are accepted as literally true.

The reasons for the long persistence record are partly understood. Ritual creates embodied memory that persists when written records are lost. Narrative creates inter-generational comprehension by giving each generation a story it can pass to the next in a form that survives translation across changing conditions. Community creates mutual reinforcement that protects individual adherence against drift. Succession protocols create institutional persistence beyond the lifespan of any specific leader. Theological or philosophical claims create stakes that justify the maintenance costs of all the above; adherents accept the costs of practice because they take the claims to be true and the stakes to be real. Without the stakes generated by the truth-claims, the maintenance costs of the other mechanisms are hard to justify, and the institutions tend to drift.

#### 10.4 Religion's relationship with the other domains

Religion interfaces with most of the other domains in the framework.

*Ethics.* Most religious traditions are partly ethical traditions. They take positions on what extensions of conatus are appropriate, what kinds of action sustain or undermine the extended being, and what counts as virtue and vice grounded in their metaphysics. The framework treats these contributions as input data subject to consistency-checking rather than authority-deferring.

*Aesthetics.* Religious traditions have produced a substantial fraction of humanity's aesthetic achievements: architecture, music, visual art, literature, poetry, ritual choreography. The connection is not accidental. Religious traditions have institutional time-horizons that justify investing in beautiful work; they have stable patronage structures that fund sustained craft development; their content gives artists subject matter with deep cultural resonance; and the conatus test applied to artifacts (Section 8.5) selects for beauty that can transmit across generations, which religious institutions have particular interest in maintaining.

*Politics.* Religious traditions historically shape coalition structures and political legitimacy. They are themselves coalitions, subject to the same captured-coalition failure mode (Section 9.2) as other coalitions; they interact with political coalitions through shared membership, competing membership, and explicit institutional arrangements.

*Morals.* Religious traditions typically provide situated moral guidance for daily life. The bidirectional ethics-morals feedback (Section 7.2) applies: religious moral guidance accumulates from lived experience across many generations, which is a large empirical dataset, and may be reliable in conditions similar to the conditions in which it accumulated.

The interfaces are structural rather than authoritative throughout.

#### 10.5 Functional substitutes and failure modes

When the institutions historically performing the meaning-making and transmission function weaken in a given culture, the function does not disappear. It gets distributed to other institutions, which were typically not designed for it. This is one specific case of the institution-mismatch failure mode (Section 9.3).

Several institutional forms have historically taken on portions of the religious function when explicit religion has weakened: states (providing meaning through national identity and civic ritual), markets (providing meaning through consumption and brand attachment), entertainment industries (providing meaning through narrative and shared cultural reference), social movements (providing meaning through cause and community), and political ideologies (providing meaning through systematic worldview and obligation). Each can perform parts of the religious function under specific conditions, but each has structural mismatches with the full function. Political ideologies that take on the full religious function (required vocabularies, ritual confessions of error, orthodoxy enforcement, treatment of dissent as moral failing rather than disagreement) exhibit religious-shaped behavior while typically denying that they are doing so, which makes the failure modes less recognized.

#### 10.6 What this religion section doesn't claim

The religion section does not claim that any specific religious tradition is correct or incorrect. The framework's naturalist commitment is methodological rather than ontological.

The religion section does not claim that religion is necessary for individual flourishing. Many individuals live well without religious commitment; many individuals live well with deep religious commitment; the framework does not adjudicate.

The religion section does not claim that religious institutions are uniquely qualified to perform the trans-generational meaning-making function. The function is structural; multiple institutional forms can in principle perform parts of it. The framework's claim is that the function is real, important, and currently performed by religious institutions more effectively than by most alternatives.

The religion section does not pretend that the AI question is solved. The substrate question for AI religion is structurally parallel to the substrate questions for AI ethics (Section 6.3) and AI aesthetics (Section 8.4): inherited content is real and operative; whether AI religious experience constitutes anything like the religious experience of biological humans is part of the consciousness question and remains open. The independent convergence with Magnifica Humanitas documented in Section 6.3 is particularly pointed in this register, because it is a religious document making the substrate claim about AI from inside the tradition the religion section here treats functionally; the encyclical's argument and this paper's argument arrive at the same caveat from positions that disagree about almost everything else, including whether religion makes truth-claims at all.

The religion section does not pretend to resolve all disagreement between religious traditions. The framework can identify structural similarities and structural reasons for persistence or failure without requiring any tradition to be the standard against which others are measured.

### 11. Cross-cutting consistency

The eight domains are bound by the consistency requirement: claims in each domain must fit with claims in every other domain. The requirement is most easily violated at several places.

*The is-ought interface.* Physics tells us what is; ethics tells us what an agent should do. Hume's gap is real; no purely descriptive set of claims entails a purely normative claim. The framework does not bridge the gap; it sidesteps it. What it supplies is a hypothetical imperative conditional on an agent's continuing, and it makes no claim that any agent ought to continue (Section 6.1).

*The metaphysics-epistemology interface.* The metaphysics says what is real; the epistemology says how we know. The system handles this by making both empirical: metaphysics is what survives sustained empirical investigation, and epistemology is the method that does the surviving.

*The ethics-morals interface.* Ethics is the framework; morals are situated. The two-way feedback (Section 7.2) handles consistency: moral situations that systematically conflict with the ethical framework are evidence that the framework needs revision, not just that the moral situations are wrong.

*The aesthetics-everything interface.* Aesthetic preferences that consistently conflict with conatus-aligned action are evidence that either the aesthetic preferences are miscalibrated or the action-framework is wrong. The two-way feedback between aesthetics and the other domains is what calibrates both over time.

*The individual-collective interface.* Pure individualism that ignores collective dependencies is inconsistent with the empirical fact that agents depend on collective structures. Pure collectivism that ignores individual conatus is inconsistent with the empirical fact that collectives are composed of individuals each running their own conatus. The framework rules out both extremes.

*The morals-politics interface.* Situated moral inference at the individual scale and strategic action at the collective scale have to be consistent. Game theory and mechanism design (Section 9.4) provide the formal apparatus for analyzing this interface.

*The religion-everything interface.* Religion provides meaning-making and inter-generational transmission that connects the individual and collective scales across time. Religious traditions are constrained by the same physics, metaphysics, and epistemology as the other domains.

*The trans-generational coherence requirement.* The system as a whole must be consistent across time. A configuration of claims that is internally consistent in any single generation but fails to transmit across generations is inconsistent under the trans-generational requirement.

One limitation of the consistency requirement should be recorded here rather than left for a reader to find. Consistency is a constraint on the relations among claims; it does not by itself determine what the claims are. A system can satisfy it completely and arrive at conclusions opposite to this one, if it admits different things as evidence. Advaita Vedānta is the clearest instance: it applies a strict non-contradiction criterion, defining the real as what is never sublated by any later cognition, and then, having admitted scriptural testimony and contemplative experience as independent means of knowledge, concludes that the empirical world *is* eventually sublated and demotes it to a lower order of reality. That is the same discipline applied to a wider evidence base, running in the opposite direction. It follows that the consistency requirement is not the load-bearing element of this framework. What does the load is Section 3: the restriction of the evidence base to what can be checked against physical observation, which is what determines which of the mutually consistent systems the requirement can produce is the one arrived at here. The requirement disciplines the structure; the empirical anchoring selects among structures. Claiming otherwise would credit the requirement with work it cannot do.

The tradition also supplies vocabulary for the requirement itself, which is worth noting since the requirement is not original to this framework and does not gain from appearing to be. Sanskrit philosophy names it twice. *Avirodha*, non-contradiction, titles the second of the four chapters of the *Brahmasūtra*, which is given over entirely to showing the system free of conflict with revelation, with tradition, and with reasoning, and to refuting the rival schools on their own ground. *Saṅgati*, connection or fit, is one of the five required components of an *adhikaraṇa*, the standard unit of argument: alongside the subject, the doubt, the opponent's position, and the settled conclusion, each unit must exhibit its link to the unit preceding it and to the whole. The second is the closer analogue to what this section does, since it is a positive requirement that each part demonstrate its place rather than merely the absence of conflict between parts.

### 12. Where this system has been told it was wrong

Everything above is the system working. A lens exhibited only from the side where it works is decoration, because nothing was ever at risk. This section collects the places where something pushed back, and states plainly how thin the collection is.

*The unification claim, declined.* Section 5.2 refuses the claim that the recurring iterative form reflects one underlying principle, and gives two specific obstacles for the refusal rather than leaving it as modesty. This is a real correction and it is the weakest kind available: an internal one, reached by inspecting the framework's own reasoning, not a case where the framework said something about the world and the world answered differently.

*The consistency requirement, demoted.* Section 11 records that Advaita Vedānta satisfies a strict non-contradiction criterion and arrives at the opposite conclusion, from a wider evidence base. That is an external system doing what this framework claimed as its distinguishing discipline, and reaching somewhere else. The response was to demote the requirement and name Section 3, the restriction of the evidence base, as what actually does the load-bearing work. This is the strongest entry in this section, because the pushback came from outside and cost the framework a claim it had been making about itself.

*Beauty as a theory-selection criterion.* Section 8.3 above. Aesthetic judgment, used as a guide to what is true in fundamental physics, produced a prediction that failed. The framework can say why it should have failed there, which is worth something and is not the same as having predicted it in advance.

*Capture as uniformly corrosive.* Section 7.3 above. The examination-system case does not fit the assumption, and the assumption turns out never to have been argued for.

What is *not* in this list is the important part. There is no instance anywhere in this paper of the system making a claim about something not yet observed, the observation being made, and the claim coming out wrong. Every entry above is of the form "this was never established" or "this does not fit." None is of the form "this predicted X and the world produced not-X."

So the test the framework has to pass should be stated explicitly, because it is not the test the paper has so far been arranged to meet. The test is not whether the system can be made consistent with what is known; Section 11 already concedes that consistency is cheap and that a rival system can have it too. The test is whether the framework names an observation that would come out differently depending on whether it is right. Three candidates are on offer and are set down here to be checked rather than admired.

First, the substrate caveat's calibration half (Section 6.3) predicts that a designed agent's inherited moral content degrades specifically as conditions diverge from its training distribution, and does not degrade in conditions similar to it. That is a claim about a pattern of failure with a shape, and a system whose moral performance degraded uniformly, or degraded in familiar conditions, would count against it.

Second, the taught-analysis asymmetry (Section 7.3) predicts that no amount of exposure alone produces analytic capability in a learning system, while exposure alone does produce perceptual capability. A system that acquired analysis from exposure without instruction would falsify it directly. This is the most checkable claim in the paper, and it is checkable by the engineering project the paper exists to serve.

Third, the structural mathematical layer (Section 8.3) predicts that elegance judgments track real structure where the calibrating feedback exists and fail where it does not, which is what the naturalness case shows on the failure side and what the mathematics literature would have to show on the success side. That check has not been made.

The first and third are not yet operationalized. The second is, and it is the one to run. Until more of the framework's claims are of that shape, this section stays short, and its shortness is the finding rather than an omission.

### 13. What this system doesn't claim

The system does not claim to be complete. The eight domains are necessary; whether they are sufficient is empirical.

The system does not claim to be prescriptive at the level of specific guidance. It provides the questions that any serious inquiry must address; specific answers depend on inputs the system does not provide.

The system does not analyze current events directly. The analytical apparatus can be applied to specific cases; the philosophy paper itself does not perform those applications.

The system does not claim to be original at the level of individual commitments. The individual positions (pragmatist epistemology, process realism, conatus ethics, multi-level aesthetics, captured-coalition politics, functional analysis of religion) are each drawn from existing traditions. The integration into a single consistent system is what is offered as new.

The system does not claim that academic philosophy has nothing to offer. The criticism in Section 1 is structural, about the institutional incentives that select against integrated work; it is not about the technical quality of work within subfields, which is often excellent.

The system does not claim its conclusions are independent of the author. The system was developed by the author with particular concerns, particular biases, and particular blind spots. The collaboration that produced it (see Acknowledgments) is itself part of what shaped its content. Other authors developing systems with similar commitments might produce different systems.

### 14. What remains genuinely open

Whether AI agents can develop substrate-equivalents to evolved moral, aesthetic, and religious calibration through learning, or whether content-without-substrate is a permanent feature of designed systems. The framework identifies the problem and declines to name a favoured architecture; which designs, if any, are better positioned is an engineering claim to be earned by a working system rather than asserted by a philosophy.

One structural observation bears on the question without settling it. Backpropagation requires the weights being differentiated through to be held fixed for the backward pass, which is why continuous learning in such systems is architecturally awkward rather than merely unsolved, and why deployed models are typically frozen at inference. If the calibration the substrate caveat identifies has to be acquired through sustained embodied feedback rather than installed at training time, then an architecture that cannot learn continuously is not merely behind on the problem but structurally unable to attempt it. Whether that is the right diagnosis is open; it is recorded as a structural question rather than a result.

A related negative observation, offered because it constrains the space of candidate architectures. Lateral inhibition is one of the mechanisms often proposed for importing cortical structure into artificial systems, and it does not transfer to token-based architectures. The mechanism presupposes a topology in which adjacency carries information, as it does in retinotopic, tonotopic, and columnar organization, and token embeddings in a data structure have no such adjacency for inhibition to operate across. Supplying an artificial adjacency does not recover it. Further, lateral inhibition in cortex is partly a consequence of tissue geometry, including the physical extent of the excitatory and inhibitory zones, and tissue geometry has no analogue in a data structure at all. The general point this instance illustrates is that borrowing a mechanism from sensory cortex requires the substrate conditions that made the mechanism work, and those conditions do not travel with the description of the mechanism.

How the conatus principle would extend across a possible engineered species transition in which a new cognitive architecture inherits human substrate and institutions but operates under different constraints. Whether AI conatus and human conatus would be compatible, in tension, or simply different is treated as open and consequential.

What the appropriate time-frame weights are for the multi-time-scale ethics-morals feedback. The framework provides the structure (multiple scales must be considered) without specifying the weights.

How to handle institutions whose public communication is strategic rather than honest. Section 5.4 provides the methodological response; the practical question of what to do when most institutional channels for empirical information are partially corrupted remains open.

Whether the two-way feedback structure that operates at engineering scale (estimation and control), cellular scale (predictive coding), individual scale (ethics-morals), and civilizational scale is one structural feature operating at multiple scales or multiple structures that happen to share a form. This is the unification question from Section 5.2. The framework commits to the structural observation and not to the unification; resolving the unification question requires either empirical establishment of the free energy principle as a substrate-independent law, or a separate argument that has not yet been developed. The framework's load-bearing arguments do not depend on the unification being correct.

Whether a complete mathematical formalization of the iterative inference structure can be developed across the full range of scales the framework addresses. Existing formalizations cover cellular scale (predictive coding equations) and strategic-interaction scale (game theory). A unified mathematical framework would substantially strengthen the system but has not yet been developed.

How aesthetic experience integrates with the ethics-morals structure when the aesthetic response and the deliberative judgment conflict. The framework treats aesthetic perception as one input to moral inference; it does not specify how to weight it against deliberative considerations when they diverge.

How to design defense institutions that maintain capability without being captured. Section 9.5 establishes the requirement; Section 9.2 establishes that defense institutions are themselves coalitions subject to capture. The intersection is one of the hard problems in political design and has no general solution in the framework.

How institutions that have weakened from their historical religious functions can be rebuilt or replaced. The framework identifies the functional gap that institution-mismatch failures produce but does not specify what successful replacement or restoration would require.

How to move from diagnostic clarity to prescriptive guidance. The current development sees problems more clearly than scattered intuitions can, but it does not yet say what to do about them. How solid the bridge from diagnostic to prescriptive ends up being is the central test of whether the system delivers practical value or only intellectual elegance.

These are not gaps to be papered over. They are research directions for subsequent work.

The work originates in an engineering concern rather than an academic one.
